## justice-position-no-1-supreme-court  (depth: deep)
- **Judicial vs. advocacy vs. quasi-judicial experience:** Melody is the sitting justice but has never been a trial judge; her record is as a career civil rights litigator (AG's office, DOJ) [S2][S3]. Colberg has 18 years of part-time bench service as a commissioner pro tem/arbitrator in family law [S1][S3]. Edwards has no bench service but 30+ years of appellate practice including a dozen-plus cases before this court [S1].
- **Judicial philosophy:** Melody emphasizes state courts as guarantors of rights (workplace, privacy, healthcare, education) amid federal change [S1][S3]. Edwards and Colberg both campaign on applying the constitution and statutes "as written" and depoliticizing the court [S1][S3].
- **Insider/outsider axis:** Melody carries the endorsement of every sitting justice, three governors, the AG, and organized labor; the challengers run explicitly against the gubernatorial-appointment pipeline [S1][S3].
- **Subject-matter lens:** Colberg's campaign is built around family law (including fast-tracking family law appeals); Edwards' around tax and constitutional litigation; Melody's around civil rights enforcement [S1][S3].

## justice-position-no-3-supreme-court  (depth: deep)
- **Judicial philosophy — the clearest axis:** Stevens is a self-described strict constructionist who would read statutes literally and interpret the constitution by text and original meaning; Diaz espouses "American pragmatism," law meeting "the community where they are"; Hawk stakes a middle framing of constitution, statute, and precedent "not personal views," with openness to reform [S2].
- **Level and type of bench experience:** Diaz is the only appellate judge (200+ opinions authored) and also served four years as a trial judge; Hawk and Stevens are trial judges, with Stevens claiming 200+ jury trials as lawyer and judge, and Hawk hundreds of cases presided plus appellate work from the trial bench [S1][S2].
- **Pre-bench careers point in different directions:** Diaz was a federal prosecutor who built a civil rights unit; Hawk was an indigent-defense and ACLU civil-liberties lawyer; Stevens worked both prosecution and defense, plus international justice missions in Kosovo and Afghanistan [S1][S2][S3].
- **Appointment vs. election and partisan alignment:** Stevens runs against the gubernatorial-appointment pipeline and holds Republican party recommendations; Diaz and Hawk were both appointed by Democratic governors and split the Democratic-aligned establishment (Diaz: former governors and most justices; Hawk: the sitting governor) [S1][S2][S3].
- **Bar evaluations:** Diaz reports "Exceptionally Well Qualified" ratings from multiple bar associations; no equivalent ratings were found for Hawk or Stevens in reviewed sources [S1][S2].

## justice-position-no-5-supreme-court  (depth: deep)
- **Type of judicial/legal experience.** Angelis: 26 years of large-firm
  appellate and IP litigation plus three months as a sitting justice [S1][S4].
  Miller: a 40-year Washington appellate practice (135 appeals) with no
  judicial office beyond pro tem service [S4]. Larson: 18 years as an elected
  municipal-court trial judge plus 23 years as a trial lawyer [S4]. Amamilo:
  a sitting superior-court judge handling felony, civil, family, and
  dependency dockets, formerly a public defender [S4]. Voters are effectively
  choosing which kind of experience — appellate craft, trial-bench
  management, or current trial-court judging — belongs on an appellate court.
- **Posture toward the current court.** Larson runs explicitly as a critic of
  recent decisions (e.g., calling the Blake decision ideologically motivated
  and opposing proposed bail rules); Amamilo emphasizes precedent and
  stability; Angelis emphasizes judicial independence and rule of law; Miller
  emphasizes error-correction and getting decisions right [S2][S4].
- **Bar evaluations.** Amamilo reports "Exceptionally Well Qualified" ratings
  from five minority/specialty bar associations and "Well Qualified" from the
  Latina/o Bar Association [S4]. Angelis is rated "Exceptionally Well
  Qualified" by Washington Women Lawyers [S5, see dossier]. Miller and Larson
  cite attorney peer ratings (Martindale-Hubbell AV Preeminent) rather than
  2026 judicial-evaluation ratings [S4].
- **Incumbency by appointment.** Angelis has institutional support (all
  colleagues, three governors) but a bench record of only a few months;
  challengers argue the seat should be filled by election rather than
  ratification of an appointment [S2][S4].

## justice-position-no-7-supreme-court  (depth: deep)
- **Judicial experience.** Stephens is the only candidate who has served as a
  judge — 18 years on the Supreme Court plus Court of Appeals service — and
  is the sitting Chief Justice [S1][S4]. Shelvey states he has no judicial
  experience; Merchant and Bloom likewise have none [S4].
- **Professional background of the challengers.** Merchant offers a criminal
  defense/constitutional-advocacy lens (15 years as a public defender, "never
  represented corporations, never represented the government"); Bloom offers
  a tax, securities, and business-law lens plus military service; Shelvey
  offers small-firm family/tax practice [S4].
- **Posture toward the court's recent rulings.** In Center Square Q&As, both
  Merchant and Bloom criticized the Quinn capital-gains-tax ruling, but they
  split on Blake: Merchant supported it, Bloom criticized it [S6]. Shelvey's
  pamphlet statement takes outcome-oriented positions (opposes a state income
  tax, "won't be soft on criminals"), which is itself unusual in a
  nonpartisan judicial race where candidates typically avoid prejudging
  issues [S4]. Stephens emphasizes judicial independence, access to justice,
  and deciding on law and facts [S4].
- **Scale and reach of campaigns.** Stephens has statewide institutional
  endorsements and the only substantial war chest; all three challengers are
  running low-budget campaigns, and one (Shelvey) reported no contributions
  at all [S5].
