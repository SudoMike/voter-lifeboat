## Background

Colleen Melody is the incumbent in Position 1, appointed by Governor Bob Ferguson in November 2025 to succeed retiring Justice Mary Yu; she took office January 1, 2026 [S18][S19]. The seat is on the November ballot as a 2-year unexpired term [S1]. She is 44, lives on Vashon Island, and grew up in Spokane [S9][S10]. She earned her undergraduate and law degrees at the University of Washington, both with high honors, and graduated first in her law school class [S1][S18]. Before joining the court she led the Attorney General's Office Wing Luke Civil Rights Division from its creation in 2015, and earlier spent four years as a trial attorney in the U.S. Department of Justice Civil Rights Division [S18][S19]. She has taught at the University of Washington and brought pro bono cases on behalf of immigrants and veterans [S1]. She is a member of the Supreme Court's rules committee [S12].

## Positions

**Judicial philosophy and approach**
- Her general-election pamphlet statement says state courts "must safeguard our rights with independence and impartiality," and that Washington's laws "provide broad protections in privacy, the workplace, housing, healthcare, and education" [S1].
- Told the Spokesman-Review that "State law is really the backbone of a lot of our rights right now" [S9].
- Named Justice Elena Kagan as a model for her "focus on the practical implications of the court's rulings on everyday people" [S10].
- On overruling precedent, said a prior decision "has to be legally incorrect and presently harmful, and that means harmful to more than just the parties before the court" [S10].
- On the court's nearly century-old ruling that progressive income taxation violates the state constitution, which is at issue in the coming challenge to the state's high-earners income tax: "It's an important precedent. And then, you know, really understanding the body of law that has post-dated it, too, because there have been a lot of tax cases carving out the difference between a property tax, an excise tax. Washington's tax system is complex." [S10]
- Told the Seattle Times editorial board in July: "Of course the court should stay in its lane, and not veer into policy, unnecessarily" [S12].
- Defended the single-subject rule applied in the court's natural gas initiative ruling as "a fairness requirement" that applies to legislators and initiative voters alike "so that they know what they're voting on" [S9].
- Supports nonpartisan judicial races to "avoid the appearance that this is partisan" [S9]. Earlier in the campaign she said judicial elections give "more accountability to voters" than lifetime appointments, and she has recused where judicial-conduct standards on impartiality required (as of July 2026) [S2].

**Court administration and access to justice**
- Her campaign puts weight on the court's work beyond deciding cases. She said, "We need to be talking with the judges and the court administrators on the ground in the different communities to understand what barriers they're facing and to do kind of real elbow grease solutions" [S11].
- Pamphlet: works to "improve access to justice" and says courts "must be places where victims of crime and discrimination may seek justice with safety and dignity" [S1].
- Campaign site lists judicial independence, "safety and dignity," accessible courts, and judicial integrity ("impartiality and humility") as priorities [S3].
- In July the Seattle Times editorial board wrote that she is working to bring more public involvement into the court's work, "including on bail reform." It gave no further detail on her position [S12].

## Record

**On the Supreme Court (since January 2026)**
- *In re Marriage of Hauk*, No. 104074-1 (Aug. 6, 2026): her only majority opinion among the court's 2026 published opinions listed as of October 8 [S5][S6]. In the 5-4 decision she held that, under the Child Relocation Act (RCW 26.09.405-.560), courts deciding whether the presumption in favor of relocation applies measure residential time at the point the relocation notice is served (in phased parenting plans, the phase then in effect). The Court of Appeals had counted future time under the plan; the court reversed and remanded [S6][S7]. The opinion relies partly on the statute's present-tense wording as evidence of legislative intent [S7]. Justice Whitener wrote the dissent, joined by Justices Johnson, Gordon McCloud and Mungia [S6].
- *Climate Solutions v. State*, No. 104,240-0 (Sept. 17, 2026): joined Chief Justice Stephens' 6-3 majority holding the 2024 natural-gas initiative invalid under the state constitution's single-subject requirement. Justice Mungia wrote the dissent [S8][S9].
- Of the 2026 published opinions listed as of October 8, she took part in 11. She wrote one majority opinion, signed ten majority or lead opinions, and wrote or joined no dissents or concurrences. She did not take part in the rest, most of them cases argued before she joined the court [S5].

**Before the court**
- As head of the AG's Civil Rights Division she led the successful challenge to the first Trump administration travel ban, won the initial temporary restraining order against the attempt to end birthright citizenship, and litigated to preserve DACA status for Washington residents [S18][S1].
- Recovered wages owed to workers and litigated pregnancy-discrimination and sexual-harassment cases [S1].

## Scoring notes

- `experience`: she is the incumbent but has served a short time (since January 2026), and her career before the court was in government civil rights litigation, not on a trial bench [S18][S5].
- `judicial`: her stated approach is that state courts and state law are the main protectors of individual rights, and she looks to the "practical implications" of rulings [S1][S9][S10]. Her one majority opinion, *Hauk*, is a statutory-text case: it reads the Child Relocation Act's present-tense wording. It does not show a broader interpretive approach either way [S7].
- `safety`: no documented prosecution or defense background. The only related item is the Seattle Times' mention of her work bringing public involvement into the court's bail-reform work, with no stated position [S12].
- Her institutional support is broad (every sitting justice, statewide Democratic officials, labor). She raised more money than her opponent, but outside spending for him ($49,828) is larger than outside spending for her ($15,628) [S4][S15][S16].
