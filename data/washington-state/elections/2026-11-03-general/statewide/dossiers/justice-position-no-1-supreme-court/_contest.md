## What this office does

The Washington Supreme Court is the state's court of last resort. Its nine justices are elected statewide in nonpartisan races and decide appeals that set binding precedent on the state constitution and state law. The court also oversees the procedures and administration of the lower courts [S2]. No party preference appears on the ballot for judicial races, though party organizations endorse in them [S2][S5][S6]. Position 1 is on the November ballot as a 2-year unexpired term, the remainder of retired Justice Mary Yu's term [S1][S5].

## Primary result

Colleen Melody placed first in the August 4 primary with 923,965 votes (53.03%). Scott Edwards was second with 513,394 (29.46%), and Laura Christensen Colberg, eliminated, had 305,110 (17.51%) [S3]. The Secretary of State certified primary results on August 21, 2026 [S4].

## Race dynamics

- Melody was appointed by Governor Bob Ferguson, her former boss at the Attorney General's Office, in November 2025 and took office January 1, 2026. Edwards is a Seattle tax attorney. The Seattle Times frames the race as a "Ferguson appointee" against a "tax foe" [S5].
- The new high-earners income tax is expected to reach the court. That case would test the court's nearly century-old ruling that progressive income taxation violates the state constitution, and the candidates' tax views have drawn attention as a result [S5][S6]. Edwards says that precedent "got it right." Melody calls it "an important precedent" to be read with the later case law distinguishing property and excise taxes [S5]. Edwards helped litigate the challenge to the state capital gains tax, which the court upheld. He has said recusal questions for either candidate are "premature" [S11].
- Edwards runs against the appointment pipeline: "Supreme Court justices should be responsible to the people, not to the governor." Melody's campaign stresses the court's administrative work on access barriers [S6].
- Endorsements: all sitting justices and Democratic officials and groups back Melody. The state Republican Party backs Edwards [S5][S6]. The King County Bar Association's ratings page lists neither as rated [S12]. The KCBA held a virtual Supreme Court candidate forum on September 30 [S13].
- Money (PDC, early October): Melody has raised $265,041 and spent $102,098; Edwards has raised $165,766 (including $9,900 in loans) and spent $123,427 [S8]. Independent spending through October 1: $49,828 supporting Edwards (Concerned Taxpayers of Washington State), $16,000 opposing him, and $15,628 supporting Melody [S9].

## What genuinely differentiates these candidates

- **`judicial`:** Edwards states an as-written approach ("apply the constitution and laws as written, not rewrite them") and says the court has become policy-driven [S1][S5]. Melody stresses state courts as protectors of rights and the "practical implications" of rulings [S1][S5][S7]. The candidates disagreed publicly on the September natural-gas initiative ruling, where Melody joined the majority and Edwards favored the dissent [S7]. Melody's one majority opinion so far (*In re Marriage of Hauk*) is a statutory-text decision and does not settle the question either way [S10].
- **`experience`:** Melody is a short-tenure incumbent (since January 2026) whose earlier career was government civil rights litigation. Edwards is an outsider with 30+ years in private appellate and tax practice and no time on the bench [S1][S5].
- **`safety`:** neither candidate has a documented prosecution, defense, bail or diversion record, so this axis does not separate them [S1][S2].
- **Subject-matter lens:** tax and constitutional litigation for Edwards, civil-rights enforcement for Melody. Edwards' tax work bears directly on the expected income-tax case [S1][S5][S11].
