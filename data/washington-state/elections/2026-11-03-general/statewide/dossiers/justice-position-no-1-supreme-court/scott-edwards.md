## Background

Scott Edwards, 60, of Bainbridge Island, is a tax and constitutional attorney in private practice [S5][S6]. His pamphlet lists Ballard Spahr/Lane Powell (2010-present; practice chair 2010-2024; board 2017-2019 and 2023-24), Perkins Coie (1999-2010) and Bogle & Gates (1996-1999) [S1]. He has been an adjunct professor at the University of Washington School of Law since 2002, teaching state and local tax [S1][S4]. He holds a UW law degree with honors (1991) and an economics degree magna cum laude from Princeton (1988), where he received the Paul Volcker Award [S1][S4]. He grew up in Anchorage; his father was Alaska attorney general (1968-1970) and U.S. Attorney (1971-1978) [S4]. He has never held judicial office [S2]. He says he decided to run because of "a real decline in the quality of decisions we're seeing coming out of the Supreme Court" [S5].

## Positions

**Judicial philosophy and approach**
- Pamphlet: "The role of the Supreme Court is to apply the constitution and laws as written, not rewrite them." It also says Washington "deserves justices who will apply the law predictably, as written, and without favoring any political agenda," and closes with "Take politics out of the courtroom" [S1].
- In June: "The role of a judge is to interpret the law as written and in accordance with the language of the Constitution, as written," and "You cannot inject your policy views" [S8].
- Says some current justices are "more focused on policy considerations rather than applying the law as written" [S6]. In July the Seattle Times editorial board wrote that he believes the court has moved from following precedent toward "legislative-like policy" but "couldn't furnish much evidence" of it [S13].
- Named former U.S. Chief Justice John Marshall as a model for "careful legal analysis, clarity in explaining decisions, and respect for the distinct roles" of the branches of government [S6].
- On overruling precedent: "The original decision has to have been wrongly decided and that wrong decision has to have been harmful" [S6].
- On the court's nearly century-old ruling against progressive income taxation, which the challenge to the new high-earners income tax will test: "Income clearly meets the definition of property in the state constitution. You either own your income or nobody does. I don't think there's any question that the court got it right." [S6]
- Said of the September natural-gas initiative ruling: "I would hope that if I had been on the court, the dissenting view would have been the majority." That was Justice Mungia's dissent, which would have upheld much of the initiative [S5].
- On judicial selection: "Supreme Court justices should be responsible to the people, not to the governor." He said constitutional elections exist because appointed justices may share the philosophy of whoever appointed them [S7]. He also said his opponent "believes that half of the job is being an advocate to the Legislature and an advocate for other things" [S7].
- Campaign site principles: independence ("never by politics or pressure"), rule of law through "clear, principled reasoning," due process and equal treatment, and litigation experience [S3].

**Tax practice and recusal**
- He has brought cases to the Supreme Court challenging the state's capital gains tax and Seattle's payroll tax on high salaries [S6]. He says, "I helped litigate the Quinn case, which upheld Washington's capital gains tax," and argues the income tax lacks the excise-tax structure the court relied on in that case [S8]. The Seattle Times describes him as a tax attorney "who has sued unsuccessfully to block progressive taxes" [S6].
- On recusal, he has said other people will argue that his tax experience, "particularly my experience challenging the capital gains tax, would be cause for me to recuse myself." He called recusal questions for either candidate "premature," to be "dealt with if and when that is something that comes before the court" [S8].

## Record

- More than 30 years representing clients at all levels of Washington's courts, including more than a dozen Washington Supreme Court cases, largely on tax and constitutional issues [S1][S8].
- Professional rankings he lists: Chambers USA (Tax, Washington) 2020-2025, Best Lawyers in America (Tax Law 2012-2026; Litigation & Controversy - Tax 2024-2026), and Washington Super Lawyers (Tax) 2012-2024 [S3][S4].
- Memberships he lists: Washington State Bar Association, American Bar Association, Council on State Taxation [S1].
- No judicial or quasi-judicial service, so there are no rulings to review [S1][S2].

## Scoring notes

- `judicial`: he states an as-written interpretive approach in the pamphlet, his campaign Q&A and news interviews. He names Marshall as a model, sides with the dissent in the initiative case, and says the court's income-tax precedent "got it right" [S1][S6][S8][S5]. His opponent instead describes her approach in terms of rights protection and practical effects [S6].
- `experience`: an outsider candidate with 30+ years of appellate and tax litigation and no time on the bench. He runs against the governor-appointment pipeline [S1][S7].
- `safety`: no documented criminal-law, prosecution, defense, bail or diversion record. His practice is civil tax and constitutional litigation [S1][S8].
- His tax practice, including the capital-gains challenge, bears directly on the coming income-tax case, and he acknowledges recusal questions may be raised [S6][S8].
- He has the state Republican Party's support and a business-oriented donor base, and more outside spending in his support ($49,828) than his opponent has ($15,628), along with $16,000 spent against him [S5][S7][S11].
