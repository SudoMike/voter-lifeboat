## Background

Debra L. Stephens is the Chief Justice of the Washington State Supreme Court and has been a justice since 2008 [S1]. Gov. Christine Gregoire appointed her to the court in 2007 [S6]. She was elected to the court in 2008, 2014 and 2020 [S7]; she ran unopposed in 2020 (98%) and received 78% in 2014, the last time she had a challenger [S6]. Her current tenure as Chief Justice began in January 2025; she also served as Chief Justice in 2020, a term that included the court's COVID-19 response [S2][S7].

Before joining the Supreme Court she was a judge on the Court of Appeals in Eastern Washington (2007), and from 1993 to 2007 she was an appellate attorney and adjunct professor at Gonzaga University [S1]. Her pamphlet statement describes her as a former constitutional law professor and the first woman from Eastern Washington on the court [S1]. She holds a J.D. and B.A. from Gonzaga University and graduated from West Valley High School in Spokane [S1][S6]. She served on the Orchard Prairie School District board in Spokane and describes herself as a former school board chair [S1][S9].

Other listed roles: co-chair of the Board for Judicial Administration; co-chair of the National Association of Women Judges' Judicial Independence Committee; member of the Chief Justices Conference Executive Committee; director of the National Courts & Sciences Institute; National Judicial College instructor; WA Civic Learning Council; former Spokane Community College instructor [S1]. Listed community service includes Upward Bound mentor, the Minority & Justice Commission, YMCA Youth & Government, Sacred Heart Children's Hospital, Habitat for Humanity, past Rotary president, and the Washington Center for Performing Arts board [S1].

**Primary result (Aug 4, 2026):** Stephens finished first of four with 938,584 votes (54.50%), ahead of Todd Bloom (468,347; 27.20%), Karim Merchant (207,603; 12.05%) and David Shelvey (107,635; 6.25%), per The Spokesman-Review's results table [S7]. News reports give her share as 54.4% to 54.5% [S6][S8]. The Secretary of State results site could not be loaded for this refresh, so the certified figures were not checked directly.

## Positions

Judicial philosophy (stated):

- Describes her approach as "judicial humility and a deep respect for text and structure": the words of a constitution or statute "matter," and the court should give them their ordinary meaning or explain when context requires a specific meaning "to effectuate its purposes" [S9].
- Says constitutional provisions "were written in broad terms precisely because their drafters understood they were creating a framework for a future they could not fully anticipate," and that the court must apply that framework "with fidelity to its purposes" [S9].
- Says Washington's state constitution "often provides broader protections than its federal counterpart," that appellate review is bound by the trial record, and that the court should weigh "the impacts of the legal question" and give clear, workable guidance to lower courts [S9].
- On precedent: calls it "the bedrock of a stable judicial system," but says that under the court's precedent a prior decision should be overruled if it is "clearly demonstrated to be both incorrect and harmful," and that precedent no longer aligned with the constitution's core protections "must yield" [S9].
- Names Justice William Brennan as a justice she admires, citing "his insistence that the Constitution's promises must be made real in people's lives" and the view that "rights without remedies are hollow" [S9].
- In October 2026 she said the court reviews initiatives against constitutional requirements the same way it reviews legislation, and that the single-subject rule ensures voters and legislators "know what they're voting on" [S6].
- Responding to claims that rulings reduce to ideology, she said such critics often "don't necessarily read what the courts have written" [S14].
- Pamphlet statement: decisions "must be based on the law and the facts"; she works to maintain the courts' "integrity and independence" in "an era of growing attempts to politicize the judiciary"; and she cites a record of "protecting individual rights" [S1].

Access to justice and court administration:

- Names "ensuring equitable access to justice and modernizing our legal system" as the courts' most pressing challenge, including keeping virtual hearings and electronic resources available to rural, low-income and self-represented litigants, and eliminating systemic bias within the judicial branch [S9].
- Says "the legal system belongs to everybody, and it needs to be accessible to everybody," and commits to continued work for self-represented litigants and people who need legal assistance [S6].
- Pamphlet cites a record of "expanding access to justice, improving efficiency and transparency" [S1].

Criminal-justice formation: the sources describe her pre-bench career as appellate practice and law teaching [S1][S6]. None of the sources reviewed document prosecution or defense work, or positions on bail, diversion or therapeutic courts.

## Record

- **Climate Sols. v. State, No. 104,240-0 (filed Sept. 17, 2026):** she wrote the majority opinion in a 6-3 decision affirming a King County Superior Court ruling that Initiative 2066 (2024, natural gas) is unconstitutional in its entirety under the single-subject rule [S4][S10]. Justices González, Montoya-Lewis, Whitener and Melody and Justice Pro Tem Madsen signed the majority; Justice Mungia wrote the dissent, joined by Justices Johnson and Gordon McCloud; Justice Angelis did not participate [S4]. The opinion found the measure combined provisions "with markedly distinct purposes and regulatory effects" that could not be severed [S10]. She later said "nothing in that opinion" questions the policy choice voters made [S6].
- **McCleary v. State, No. 84362-7 (2012):** wrote the majority opinion holding that article IX, section 1 of the state constitution gives children a positive right to amply funded K-12 education and that the State was not meeting that duty [S2][S6]. In 2026 she said the court "was very clear that we were not gonna tell the state how much to do this," and that retaining jurisdiction to monitor compliance was "a very responsible thing" given what happened after the 1978 school-funding case [S6].
- On the Supreme Court continuously since January 2008, with two tenures as Chief Justice [S1][S2].
- Co-chairs the Board for Judicial Administration [S1].
- At the Seattle Times endorsement interview, asked whether the court's Quinn capital-gains decision opens the door to upholding the new income tax, she answered "Not at all" [S11].

## Scoring notes

- **Experience:** the only candidate in the race who has been a judge: 18 years on the Supreme Court, the current Chief Justice, and earlier Court of Appeals service. Her opponent has never held judicial office [S1][S6]. The Seattle Times endorsement rested on her institutional knowledge during a year of turnover on the court [S11].
- **Judicial:** she describes her method as textual ("respect for text and structure") and also says constitutional provisions are a broad framework applied "with fidelity to its purposes," that the state constitution can give broader protection than the federal one, and that remedies must make rights real [S9]. Her authored record includes McCleary, which recognized a positive constitutional right and kept the court involved in enforcing it, and the 2026 ruling striking down I-2066 on single-subject grounds [S2][S4][S6]. Her opponent criticizes both decisions [S6].
- **Safety:** no documented prosecution or defense background and no documented bail, diversion or therapeutic-court positions. The sources give no evidence on this axis [S1][S6].
- **Institutional support and resources:** every other sitting justice and 150+ judges endorse her, and she has raised about five times what her opponent has [S3][S5].
