## What the office does

The Washington State Supreme Court is the state's highest court. It hears everything from criminal cases to civil lawsuits, including challenges to state laws, and it guides the work of the lower courts [S3][S5]. The court has nine justices. Each is either appointed by the governor to fill a vacancy or elected by voters to a six-year term in a nonpartisan race [S1][S3]. Position 7 is held by the Chief Justice, who presides over the court, co-chairs the Board for Judicial Administration, and receives the top of the justices' salary range ($273,819, against $269,924 for associate justices) [S1][S2][S3].

## Primary result

Four candidates ran in the August 4, 2026 primary. Chief Justice Debra L. Stephens received 938,584 votes (54.50%), Todd A. Bloom 468,347 (27.20%), Karim A. Merchant 207,603 (12.05%) and David R. Shelvey 107,635 (6.25%), per The Spokesman-Review's results table [S4]. Stephens and Bloom advanced to the general election [S4][S5]. News stories give slightly different shares (Stephens 54.4% to 54.5%, Bloom 27.1% to 27.5%) [S5][S6]. The Secretary of State results site could not be loaded for this refresh, so these numbers are not confirmed as certified.

## Race dynamics

This is the only one of 2026's five Supreme Court races with a previously elected justice on the ballot. The Washington State Standard calls it "the least competitive of the bunch" [S5]. Stephens has been on the court since 2008. She ran unopposed in 2020 and won 78% of the vote against a challenger in 2014 [S5][S6]. Bloom is a Tacoma tax attorney, CPA and Navy veteran. He has run before: for the U.S. House as a Republican in 2016 and 2022, and for Supreme Court Position 2 in 2024, when he took 16% and did not advance [S6][S15].

The two candidates draw on very different sources of support. Stephens is endorsed by all eight of her colleagues on the court, more than 150 judges, Gov. Bob Ferguson, House Speaker Laurie Jinkins and Senate Majority Leader Jamie Pedersen [S7][S13]. The state Republican Party recommended Bloom [S7][S14]. The Washington State Standard describes the five 2026 court races together as proxy contests in which GOP-backed challengers face candidates favored by Democratic leaders [S7]. Under the judicial rules, these endorsements are recorded as facts only and say nothing about either candidate's judicial philosophy. The Seattle Times endorsed Stephens in the primary, calling both challengers it interviewed "qualified and thoughtful" [S10]. PDC records show Stephens with $136,055.50 in contributions and $15,627.65 in supporting independent expenditures as of Oct. 5, and Bloom with $28,604.46 as of Oct. 2 [S8][S9]. The King County Bar Association had not rated either candidate as of its August 4 update [S11].

General-election coverage has focused on two rulings Stephens wrote. One is the September 17, 2026 6-3 decision striking down voter-approved Initiative 2066 (natural gas) under the single-subject rule; Bloom calls it an example of "two-tier justice" [S6][S12]. The other is the 2012 McCleary school-funding decision, whose contempt sanctions against the Legislature Bloom calls an overreach of the separation of powers [S6].

## What genuinely differentiates the candidates

- **Experience (record vs. renewal):** Stephens has 18 years on the Supreme Court, is the current Chief Justice, and served earlier on the Court of Appeals. Bloom has never been a judge; he points to FINRA arbitration, Navy disciplinary duties and service on the WSBA Board of Governors, and frames the race as a choice between "the current justices" and "someone new" [S1][S6][S15].
- **Judicial philosophy:** Bloom describes a restraint and separation-of-powers approach ("apply the law as written") and disputes McCleary's remedy, the I-2066 ruling and the Quinn capital-gains decision. He also says rulings are not made "in a vacuum" and that the court should respect voter-approved measures more [S3][S6]. Stephens describes "respect for text and structure" applied to constitutional provisions written as a broad framework. She says the state constitution often gives broader protection than the federal one, and she defends McCleary's ongoing oversight as accountability [S3][S6].
- **Public safety:** neither candidate has a documented prosecution or criminal-defense background, and neither has documented positions on bail, diversion or therapeutic courts. The sources do not separate them on this axis [S1][S6][S15].
- **Not differentiating:** both candidates use the language of impartiality, independence and public trust ("fair and impartial," "uphold the rule of law"). That boilerplate does not distinguish them [S1][S3].
