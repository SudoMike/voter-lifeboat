## What the office does

The Washington State Supreme Court is the state's court of last resort: nine justices who decide appeals from the Courts of Appeals, direct-review cases, questions of state constitutional law, and attorney-discipline matters, and who adopt the rules governing all state courts [S2]. Justices are elected statewide in nonpartisan races [S2]. Position 5 is on this ballot for a 2-year unexpired term, the remainder of retired Justice Barbara Madsen's seat [S1][S2]. News coverage frames the 2026 cycle around turnover on the court and pending litigation over the state's new income tax on high earners [S5].

## Primary result

Four candidates ran in the August 4, 2026 primary. Per Secretary of State data published by the Spokesman-Review (last updated August 21): Theo Angelis 623,396 (35.92%), Dave Larson 559,629 (32.24%), Sharonda Amamilo 371,059 (21.38%), Greg Miller 181,542 (10.46%) [S3]. Angelis and Larson advanced to the general [S3].

## Race dynamics

- Appointee against a returning challenger. Angelis was appointed by Governor Ferguson and joined the court April 4, 2026; Larson is a retired Federal Way Municipal Court judge who lost the 2024 Supreme Court race by about 20,000 votes [S4][S5].
- Appointment and independence are the central argument. Spokesman-Review coverage centers on "suggested partisanship": Angelis's past professional and personal ties to Ferguson, and Larson's proposal for a bipartisan Judicial Elections Commission to vet appointees, which he presents as a response to the appearance of "cronyism" [S4][S6]. Angelis says rulings cannot be predicted from who appointed a justice, and that he lists his potential biases for every case [S4][S6].
- Endorsements split along party-organization lines, though the office is nonpartisan. Larson has Republican Party recommendations and Forward Party backing; Angelis has Democratic organizations and labor [S4][S5]. Newspapers: the Seattle Times endorsed Angelis for the general after backing Amamilo in the primary [S14].
- Money is close. PDC data through October 1 show about $435,600 in contributions for Angelis and $405,100 for Larson [S8]. Before the primary, Concerned Taxpayers of Washington State spent about $50,000 supporting Larson, and Beware the Attack on Our Courts spent about $16,000 opposing him [S5].

## What genuinely differentiates the candidates

- `judicial`: Larson gives an explicit as-written statement ("Apply the law as written, not as you wish it had been") and has criticized the court's Blake decision as ideological [S2][S9]. Angelis's statements describe neutral application of law and setting aside policy preferences, and he has no authored opinions: a review of Supreme Court opinions filed through October 1, 2026 found none written by him, and he did not participate in most cases decided since he joined [S7][S10]. Neither candidate has an appellate record to compare.
- `safety`: Larson's formation is 18 years on a municipal criminal and infraction docket, including service on the DMCJA Therapeutic Courts Committee and opposition to proposed bail rules [S2]. Angelis's formation is civil appellate and IP litigation plus pro bono immigration work, with no documented bail, diversion, or therapeutic-court positions [S1][S2].
- `experience`: Angelis runs as the incumbent with about six months on the bench and the backing of all his colleagues; Larson runs on trial-bench experience and on changing how justices are appointed [S1][S4][S6].
- Ratings: Angelis is rated "Exceptionally Well Qualified" by Washington Women Lawyers and the Joint Asian Judicial Evaluations Committee for 2026 [S12][S13]. Larson's pamphlet claims an "Exceptionally Well Qualified" rating without naming the rater; the only 2026 rating located for him is "Qualified" from the Tacoma-Pierce County Bar Association [S1][S11].
