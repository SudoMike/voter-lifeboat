## What it does

Initiative Measure No. IP26-645 is an initiative to the people on the November 3, 2026 statewide ballot; its ballot title reads: "This measure would repeal a 9.9% tax on annual individual income over $1,000,000; prohibit taxes measured by individual income and taxes on individual income or the receipt of individual income; and define 'income.'" [S1] The ballot also carries the Attorney General's public investment impact disclosure: "This measure would decrease funding for public K-12 education, higher education (including universities and community colleges), and human services (primarily healthcare)." [S1][S6] The House Office of Program Research records that the initiative was certified to the ballot on July 22, 2026 [S7]. If approved, it takes effect 30 days after the election [S7]; OFM gives the effective date as December 3, 2026 [S3].

The measure does two things:

1. **Repeal.** It repeals chapter 238, Laws of 2026 (ESSB 6346), the 9.9% tax on Washington taxable income over $1,000,000, by repealing that act's income-tax sections: the tax itself, the distribution of its revenue, the computation of Washington base and taxable income, allocation and apportionment, estimated payments, crimes, administration, the pension-income amendments (2026 c 238 ss 801-814), the act's intent section (s 1) and its amendment of Initiative 2111 (s 1201) [S5][S7].
2. **Prohibition.** It adds a new chapter to Title 82 RCW stating that "No tax may be imposed on individual income, the receipt of individual income, or measured by an individual's income, regardless of the source," that "Income is subject to individual ownership," and that "The prohibition in this section applies to the state, counties, cities, and all other local jurisdictions in the state of Washington" [S5]. It defines "income" as "any gain or benefit measured in money derived from an individual's capital, labor, property, or other source" and "individual" as "a natural person," for all of Title 82 unless the context clearly requires otherwise [S5][S7]. The prohibition sections are "to be liberally construed to prohibit any form of individual income tax" [S5].

The initiative's findings state that "income is property" under the state constitution and "cannot be taxed unless it meets all constitutional requirements for property taxes including a uniform rate not exceeding 1 percent," and that voters have rejected "10 attempts to impose a statewide income tax" [S5].

The repeal list in section 2 of the initiative names sections 1, 101-102, 201-206, 301-316, 401-407, 501-502, 601, 701-712, 801-814, 1201, 1203 and 1208-1210 of 2026 c 238; it does not list sections 901 through 1107, the act's sales-tax, B&O tax and working families tax credit changes [S5][S8]. The Attorney General's explanatory statement says: "The non-income tax credits, exemptions, and deductions that accompanied this tax would not be repealed and would remain in place." [S2]

## The law being repealed (ESSB 6346, chapter 238, Laws of 2026)

- **Enactment.** ESSB 6346 passed the House 51-46 on March 9, 2026 and the Senate 27-21 on March 11, 2026; Governor Bob Ferguson approved it March 30, 2026 and it was filed March 31, 2026 [S8]. The act's general effective date is June 11, 2026, with listed sections taking effect July 1, 2026 or January 1, 2029 [S8].
- **The tax.** "Beginning January 1, 2028, a tax is imposed on the receipt of Washington taxable income. Only individuals are subject to payment of the tax, which equals 9.90 percent multiplied by an individual's Washington taxable income." (sec. 201) [S8] The first payments are due in calendar year 2029 [S7]; OFM assumes first payments in April 2029 [S3].
- **The $1 million threshold: individual vs. household.** Section 314 of the bill allows "a standard deduction of $1,000,000 per individual, or in the case of spouses or state registered domestic partners, their combined standard deduction is $1,000,000, regardless of whether they file joint or separate returns" [S8]. So a single filer pays on income above $1 million, and a married couple or registered domestic partnership shares one $1 million deduction [S8][S7]. The Attorney General's statement describes this as applying "per household" [S2]; OFM's fiscal statement describes the tax as applying to income "greater than $1,000,000 per individual" [S3]; the ballot title says "annual individual income over $1,000,000" [S1]. The bill's intent section speaks of limiting the tax "to households with annual adjusted gross income of $1,000,000 or more" [S8]. The deduction is indexed to inflation every other year beginning October 2029 (sec. 316) [S8].
- **What is taxed.** Washington taxable income starts from federal adjusted gross income, with Washington modifications, a charitable deduction of up to $100,000 (shared by spouses or domestic partners) and the $1 million standard deduction [S7][S8]. Residents are taxed on all income; nonresidents only on Washington-source income [S2][S7][S8]. Credits are allowed for income tax paid to another state, for Washington capital gains tax paid, and for B&O or public utility tax paid on the same income [S2][S7].
- **Who pays.** OFM's estimate assumes DOR administration "for approximately 30,000 taxpayers" [S3]. The bill's intent section says the tax "is estimated to affect only the wealthiest one-half of one percent of the households in this state" [S8].
- **Where the money goes.** Section 202 deposits collections in the state general fund "to fund the sales and use tax relief ..., the working families' tax credit program, ... and the business and occupation tax relief ..., and to make public investments in K-12 education, health care, human services, and higher education"; beginning July 1, 2029, 5 percent of the prior fiscal year's collections goes to the fair start for kids account (child care and early learning) [S8][S2]. OFM notes the Legislature "expressed an intent to use the additional revenue ... towards providing free school meals, transferring funds for childcare and early learning, and transferring funds to local governments to mitigate sales tax loss" [S3].
- **Accompanying tax cuts.** The same act exempted diapers, over-the-counter drugs, grooming and hygiene products and some software, digital services, data processing and live presentations from sales tax; increased a small-business B&O credit and filing threshold; removed a B&O surcharge for several industries; and expanded the Working Families Tax Credit [S2]. The act's intent section (sec. 1(11)) said the Legislature intended the tax and these reductions to "operate together ... as an integrated reform," and that "repeal or invalidation of section 201 of this act would reinstate certain sales and use tax on items made exempt by this act and repeal working families tax credits and small business tax credits" [S8]. The act's null-and-void clause (sec. 1202) is triggered "If a court of final jurisdiction invalidates section 201" [S8]. The initiative repeals section 1 (the intent section) and does not list section 1202 [S5].
- **Initiative 2111.** In 2024 the Legislature passed Initiative 2111, which "prohibits the state, counties, cities, and other local jurisdictions from imposing taxes on any form of individual income" [S7]. ESSB 6346 section 1201 amended that law (RCW 1.90.100) to add that the ban "does not apply to the tax authorized in chapter 82A.04 RCW so long as the standard deduction is at least $1,000,000 for a household" [S8][S9].

## What the local-tax ban forecloses

- Under the initiative, neither the state nor any county, city or other local jurisdiction could impose a tax on individual income, on the receipt of it, or measured by it, from any source [S5][S7]. That covers a flat or graduated income tax of any size, at either level of government, as a matter of statute [S5].
- Local governments are already barred by statute: RCW 1.90.100(1), from Initiative 2111, says "Neither the state nor any county, city, or other local jurisdiction in the state of Washington may tax any individual person on any form of personal income," and ESSB 6346 exempted only its own state tax from that ban [S9][S7]. The initiative's new prohibition restates that ban for local governments in Title 82, with its own broader definition of "income" ("any gain or benefit measured in money ...") and a "measured by" clause, rather than I-2111's reference to federal "gross income" [S5][S9].
- Before Initiative 2111, a 1984 statute barring city and county net income taxes (chapter 36.65 RCW) had been held unconstitutional by the Court of Appeals in Kunath v. City of Seattle (2019), which also held Seattle's graduated income tax unconstitutional under the state constitution's uniformity requirement [S10].
- The Attorney General states that "The only tax explicitly listed and repealed by the measure is the tax on income over $1,000,000." [S2] OFM's fiscal estimate assumes "The only tax impacted is the tax described in ESSB 6346" [S3]. Neither source analyzes whether the new definition of "income" affects any other existing tax [S2][S3].

## Arguments for

From the statement in favor, written by Chris Gildon (State Senator, Republican, Puyallup), Jesse Proudman, Patti Mann (retired Seattle Fire Department firefighter/paramedic), Viet Nguyen, Semmelle Abraham and Julie H Olds [S4]:

- Washington voters "have rejected an income tax ten times," and the Legislature "enacted a new income tax anyway"; the initiative "restores your right to decide" [S4].
- "Taxes Always Expand": the statement says lawmakers "rejected opportunities to permanently limit its reach, giving Olympia the ability to expand the income tax to everyone" [S4].
- It cites "80 times in the last five years" that the Legislature moved money from dedicated accounts to the general fund, and capital gains tax commitments to early learning and childcare that it says were "delayed, reduced, or cancelled" [S4].
- It says a yes vote "repeals the new income tax before it begins, preserves the Working Families Tax Credit, paid family leave, and small-business tax relief" [S4].
- Rebuttal: "Without I-645, Olympia can expand the income tax to reach into your paycheck with the simple stroke of a pen"; "Washington has record revenues" [S4].

## Ballot language litigation (context)

- The July 2026 lawsuits challenged the one-sentence public investment impact disclosure, not the ballot title [S15]. A 2022 law requires such a disclosure of 15 words or less on the ballot when a measure repeals, levies or modifies a tax or fee and would cause a net change in state revenue [S15].
- Let's Go Washington petitioned Thurston County Superior Court on July 27, 2026, arguing the disclosure "is inaccurate and would create prejudice against the measure" and that the initiative would in fact increase budget funding [S15]. Separately, Arthur West sued in the same court seeking an injunction to keep the disclosure off ballots [S15].
- On August 7, 2026, Judge Chris Lanese upheld the statute in West's case, concluding the statement "ensures voters are aware of trade-offs" [S18][S17][S16]. The Center Square reported that this ruling pre-empted Let's Go Washington's separate challenge, whose hearing was canceled [S16].
- State Supreme Court Commissioner Michael Johnston denied West's request for a preliminary injunction; the Washington State Republican Party, Tim Eyman and the Citizens in Charge Foundation filed briefs supporting West, and the Washington State Budget and Policy Center and the Economic Opportunity Institute filed a brief opposing him [S17].
- On September 3, 2026, the state Supreme Court deadlocked 4-4 on West's emergency motion, so the motion was denied and the disclosure stays on the ballot [S18]. West's broader challenge to the disclosure law was still pending as of that report [S18].
- The ballot title and disclosure appear as the Attorney General drafted them [S1][S6].

## Axis notes

- `taxes`: a yes vote repeals a 9.9% tax on income over $1 million, an estimated $11.4 billion in state revenue over SFY 2027-2031, and bars any state or local income tax by statute [S1][S3][S5]. This is a direct choice between the rubric's "limit & cut taxes" pole and its "millionaires tax" example of the progressive-revenue pole.
- `local-control`: the prohibition applies the same rule to "the state, counties, cities, and all other local jurisdictions" [S5]. A yes vote sets a statewide limit on local taxing authority; the practical change for local governments is small because RCW 1.90.100 (I-2111) already bars local income taxes [S9][S7].
- `spending`: the yes argument centers on fund transfers and broken spending commitments [S4]; the no argument centers on funding for schools, health care and childcare [S4]; OFM says the revenue loss would reduce general fund support for K-12, higher education and human services [S3].
- Neither argument addresses parental rights, gender policy or other axes [S4].
