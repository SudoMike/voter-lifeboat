## What this office does

The Washington Supreme Court is the state's court of last resort: nine justices elected statewide in nonpartisan races to six-year terms decide appeals that set binding precedent on the state constitution and statutes, and the court oversees administration of the lower courts [S1][S2]. Beyond deciding cases, the justices set court rules and the direction of the court system; recent examples cited in coverage include cutting public-defender caseload limits and upcoming consideration of bail-bond reforms [S6]. Judicial candidates are barred from discussing legal issues that could come before the court [S6].

## Primary result

Position 3 is an open seat: Justice Raquel Montoya-Lewis did not seek re-election [S5][S7]. In the Aug. 4 primary, certified by the Secretary of State on Aug. 21, 2026, Mason County Superior Court Judge David Stevens finished first with 612,267 votes (35.29%), King County Superior Court Judge Jaime Michelle Hawk second with 578,979 (33.37%), and Court of Appeals Judge Mike Diaz third with 543,721 (31.34%); Stevens and Hawk advance [S3][S4]. Coverage noted that Hawk and Diaz split the Democratic-backed vote [S5].

## Race dynamics

Both finalists are sitting superior court judges with Spokane ties: Hawk was a federal defender in Spokane and taught at Gonzaga Law; Stevens was a Spokane County deputy prosecutor for about a decade [S5]. The 2026 Supreme Court races are described as statewide proxy contests, with Republican-endorsed challengers against candidates favored by Democratic leaders; Stevens is recommended by the state Republican Party, while Hawk is endorsed by Governor Ferguson, most current justices, and labor and Democratic organizations [S5][S6]. Both candidates have criticized the politicization of the race while each holds party-aligned endorsements [S5]. Fundraising is uneven: per PDC data, Hawk's committee reported $310,577.52 in contributions (record updated Oct. 2) versus $74,433.50 for Stevens (updated Oct. 5), though Stevens has $49,719.30 in independent expenditures supporting him compared with $13,627.65 for Hawk [S8][S9]. As of Oct. 8, the King County Bar Association ratings page listed neither candidate as rated ("N/A") [S10].

## What genuinely differentiates these candidates

- **Judicial philosophy (`judicial`):** Stevens describes himself as a textualist and originalist who says courts should not "lead the way" on policy; Hawk describes a text-then-legislative-intent method and says precedent may be overturned, cautiously, when "incorrect and or wrongfully decided and harmful," citing recent racial-justice-related reversals as examples [S5][S6].
- **Criminal-justice formation and court rules (`safety`):** Stevens has a long prosecution career plus defense work, and openly criticizes the court's recent criminal-procedure rule changes (juvenile warrants, arraignment deadlines, bail proposals, jury-selection rules, the mental health sentencing alternative for firearm cases); Hawk's background is public defense and the ACLU, and her stated priorities are court funding, juror pay, and access to counsel in family law [S5][S6][S7].
- **Appointment vs. election (`experience`):** Hawk was appointed by Governor Inslee in 2022 and then elected, and holds the endorsements of the sitting governor and most current justices; Stevens won his seat by defeating an appointed incumbent in 2023 and campaigns against the appointment pipeline [S5][S6][S7].
- Neither candidate has appellate-bench experience; both are trial judges [S5].
