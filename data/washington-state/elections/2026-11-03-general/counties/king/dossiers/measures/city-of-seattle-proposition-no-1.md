## What it does

Proposition No. 1 asks City of Seattle voters to authorize "a 0.3% sales and use tax for up to ten years to replace the current voter-approved 0.15% sales tax expiring March 31, 2027" [S1]. The money would go to "transit services benefiting Seattle residents, including more frequent service; affordable fare programs for qualifying individuals; reliability, access, and climate projects; enhanced service on RapidRide and routes serving regional centers and high equity priority areas; facilitation of Sound Transit expansion; and others as provided in Ordinance 127474" [S1]. Passage requires a simple majority [S1].

Under Section 1 of Ordinance 127474, the tax would take effect no earlier than April 1, 2027 and run for up to ten years, ending March 31, 2037 [S3][S4]. The explanatory statement says a future ordinance would actually impose the tax, at no more than 0.3 percent [S1]. The ordinance calls the program the "Seattle Transit Measure" (STM) and says it is a renewal of the existing measure [S3].

Allowed uses (Section 2) [S3]:

- **2.A.1 Metro bus service hours** on King County Metro routes with more than 65 percent of stops in Seattle, prioritized with a "system-wide, data-driven, equity-centered growth-focused" approach. Night service "shall be considered an essential component of all day transit access" [S3].
- **2.A.2 Transit safety and security.** This covers local implementation of King County Regional Transit Safety Task Force recommendations, "including but not limited to funding for additional behavioral health specialists, Metro Transit Police (MTP) Officers, Transit Security Officers, Metro Safety Ambassadors, contracted security personnel, fare enforcement officers," above Metro's baseline [S3].
- **2.B-2.E Specific service.** Night service for teens attending City evening programs; RapidRide service hours; service connecting Regional Centers to downtown and light rail; and routes serving the highest equity priority areas [S3].
- **2.F Transit access (fare) programs.** These serve qualifying seniors, people with disabilities, residents of Seattle Housing Authority and other low-income housing, youth and young adults, students (including trade and apprenticeship students), low-income families and participants in City income-verified programs. Capped at up to $12 million a year [S3].
- **2.G Electric transit.** Planning and deploying more electric transit, including electric buses in Duwamish Valley and South Seattle communities identified as overburdened by air pollution [S3].
- **2.H Infrastructure and capital.** Transit speed, reliability, accessibility and ADA projects, capped at up to $5 million a year (excluding carryforwards and Section 4 reappropriations) [S3].
- **2.I Sound Transit 3 staffing.** City staff for planning, permitting and delivery of the West Seattle Link Extension, Ballard Link Extension and Graham St infill station, capped at up to $8 million a year [S3].

Spending floors and other guardrails:

- **75% floor (Section 3).** Appropriations under subsections 2.A through 2.E must equal at least 75 percent of annual revenue [S3]. As enacted, subsection 2.A contains both service hours (2.A.1) and the safety and security supports (2.A.2), so both count toward the floor [S3].
- **Unspent service money (Section 4).** Starting in 2028, SDOT must report each year on unspent service money. The Council states its intent to reappropriate those funds to pedestrian access and accessibility under 2.H [S3].
- **Lowering the tax (Section 5).** The City can pass an ordinance lowering the rate or changing the Section 3 floor if a countywide transit measure, new state-authorized progressive revenue, or new or expanded local progressive revenue (such as congestion pricing or a commercial parking tax) can replace the money [S3].
- **No supplanting (Section 6).** The City expects interlocal agreements with King County Metro that keep the money from supplanting existing Metro service or Metro safety and security services [S3].
- **Oversight and reports (Sections 7-13).** The Seattle Transit Advisory Board continues as the oversight committee [S1][S3]. The ordinance requires an annual public report and a separate annual SDOT/Metro report starting March 31, 2028 on fare recovery, on-time performance and reliability [S3]. It also requires one-time reports on:
  - routes near schools and community centers
  - expanding the Transit Access Program
  - bus stop spacing
  - the Waterfront Shuttle
  - smaller buses

  A midterm evaluation is due by June 30, 2032. It must include recommendations on "whether to maintain or reduce the rate" [S3].

## Cost

- **Rate.** The ballot title says: "It authorizes a 0.3% sales and use tax for up to ten years to replace the current voter-approved 0.15% sales tax expiring March 31, 2027." [S1] That doubles the current 0.15% rate [S7][S8]. The 0.3% figure is the maximum rate allowed under the City's transportation benefit district authority, and 10 years is the maximum term [S7].
- **Sales and use tax only.** The measure contains no vehicle license fee. Section 1 authorizes only "up to a 0.3 percent sales and use tax" [S3]. Asked why she did not propose a car-tab fee, Mayor Wilson said car-tab fees would be more controversial and more likely to draw organized opposition. She called a sales tax "far from a perfect tool" [S21]. Separately, the City already charges a $50 vehicle license fee that the Council imposed itself (no vote). That fee is projected to raise $22.1 million in 2026 and is not part of this measure [S7].
- **City revenue estimate.** SDOT's fiscal note estimates $138 million a year on average over ten years. It projects "slightly less than $90 million" in 2027 ($88.9 million) and $120 million in 2028, the first full year, for a total of $1.38 billion through the end in 2037 [S8]. For comparison, the current 0.15% measure is projected to raise $56.6 million in 2026 [S7].
- **Household cost (City figure).** Council Central Staff reported that "SDOT has estimated the cost of this sales tax on a two-person median income household to be $58 per year" [S7]. The Seattle Times reported the same city-staff estimate as $58 for a typical two-person household making $124,000 [S21]. That $58 is the cost of the full 0.3%, not only the increase.
- **Household cost (campaign figures).** The statement in favor says "For just $29 more per year per household" [S2]. The campaign website says "about $29 a year for the median two-person household" [S16]. The Urbanist reported the increase as an additional $29 for a median two-person household earning $121,000 [S18]. The pro rebuttal says "The measure costs a low income household $17 a year" [S2]. No City document found gives the $17 figure; it comes from the campaign statement.
- **Effect on Seattle's sales tax rate.** The Department of Revenue lists Seattle's combined rate for Q4 2026 as 10.55% (6.5% state plus 4.05% local) [S13]. Central Staff also cites 10.55% as the current rate, which includes the 0.15% Seattle Transit Measure [S7]. The Seattle Times reported the measure would take the rate from 10.55% to 10.7% [S21][S22]. The statement against cites the same 10.7% [S2].

  Separately, the King County Transportation District imposed a countywide 0.1% sales tax for roads, collected starting January 2027 [S7]. With both taxes, The Seattle Times and The Urbanist report the Seattle rate would be 10.8% [S22][S17].
- **Election cost.** The City estimates $1 million to $1.25 million, including the voters' pamphlet [S8].

## Fiscal mechanics / What it replaces

- **2014: first Seattle measure.**
  - In 2014 King County proposed a countywide 0.1% sales tax and $60 vehicle license fee for Metro and roads. County voters rejected it, though it had majority support in Seattle [S7].
  - Seattle voters then approved STBD Proposition 1 on November 4, 2014. It authorized a 0.1% sales tax and a $60 vehicle license fee, with a $20 low-income rebate, for a six-year term ending December 31, 2020 [S3]. SDOT reports it passed with 62% [S8] and raised about $55 million a year [S7].
  - In 2016 the City took over the Seattle Transportation Benefit District's powers by Ordinance 125070 [S3].
- **2020: current measure.**
  - In July 2020 the Council sent voters Ordinance 126115, a sales-tax-only measure of up to 0.15% for six years ending March 31, 2027, with no vehicle license fee [S3][S8].
  - SDOT's fiscal note says the car-tab fee was dropped because Initiative 976 had removed TBD vehicle-fee authority. The court had not yet struck down I-976 when the 2020 filing deadline came [S8].
  - Seattle voters approved it on November 3, 2020 "by 80 percent of qualified Seattle electors" [S3]. The Seattle Times reports the share as 80.3% [S21]. The tax was imposed by Ordinance 126250 [S3]. Collections began April 1, 2021 [S8].
  - The Urbanist reports that Mayor Jenny Durkan had proposed keeping 0.1%, and Council President Lorena González's amendment raised it to 0.15% [S17][S20].
- **What the current measure funds.** It pays for about 180,000 bus trips a year, which SDOT describes as about 7% of bus service in Seattle [S7][S8]. Planned 2026 spending is about $85.2 million, which draws on fund balance:
  - $44.2 million for Metro service
  - $15.4 million for the streetcar
  - $5.7 million for low-income transit access
  - $5.2 million for ST3 staffing
  - $14.7 million for transit infrastructure [S7]
- **Why the November vote.** The Department of Revenue requires one calendar quarter of notice before a new sales tax. To start collecting April 1, 2027, the day after the current tax expires, voters must approve in November 2026 and the Council must enact the tax by December 31, 2026 [S3].
- **City's planned spending if approved** (SDOT spend plan) [S8]:
  - transit service growing from $66.9 million (2027) to $142.8 million (2036)
  - Transportation Access Program $5.5 to $10.8 million
  - ST3 staffing $5.2 to $7.1 million
  - transit infrastructure $5 million a year
  - a ramp-down reserve rising to $40.1 million
- **Service levels.** City documents and coverage describe service rising from about 180,000 to about 275,000-280,000 Metro trips a year (about 95,000-100,000 more) [S7][S16][S20]. SDOT's fiscal note describes this as "approximately 95,000 annual service hours" more [S8]. The documents use "trips" and "service hours" inconsistently [S7][S8][S17].
- **Free ORCA passes.** Passes would rise from about 10,000 to about 22,000. The Urbanist and the campaign attribute this to a renegotiated per-pass price with Metro [S17][S20][S16].
- **If it fails.** SDOT says the roughly 180,000 annual bus trips the City buys "would be eliminated by Metro's 2027 fall service change." Other STM-funded programs would also lose their funding source unless SDOT cut other programs [S8]:
  - transit pass subsidies for more than 10,000 low-income residents
  - Seattle Streetcar operations
  - 10 FTE supporting ST3
  - transit capital work

  Central Staff notes that remaining fund balance and collections through April 2027 would cover a gradual wind-down of service purchases under the City's Metro agreement [S7].
- **Implementation still needed.** Seattle buys transit service under an interlocal agreement with King County that expires at the end of 2027. A new agreement would need approval from both the City Council and the King County Council. Annual appropriations would still go through the City budget [S7].

## Arguments for

From the statement in favor, submitted by Kirk Hovenkotter, Kathryn Ricchiuto and Nick Sattele (info@hoponboardseattle.org) [S2]:

- "Seattle needs great transit. Without it, riders with disabilities, older adults, youth, and working families are stuck waiting for the bus and miss opportunities." [S2]
- The 2020 measure "has delivered: buses run more often, and 10,000 of our neighbors receive free transit passes. But this funding runs out in 2027 unless we renew it." [S2]
- "For just $29 more per year per household, Proposition 1 will ensure buses come more often, later at night, and on weekends. It will double the free passes for low income Seattleites." [S2]
- "With buses we can count on, more families can skip the expense of gas or a second car. And we can all enjoy less traffic and cleaner air." [S2]
- Rebuttal: "Voting no cuts bus service working people rely on." It also says: "The measure costs a low income household $17 a year. Owning a car costs $11,577 a year. Proposition 1 provides free ORCA cards to 22,000 of the lowest income riders." [S2]

KUOW identified Katy Ricchiuto as policy director of the Transportation Choices Coalition [S24]. The pro committee's PDC registration lists a transportationchoices.org email (kirk@) [S14].

## Arguments against

From the statement in opposition, submitted by Ari Hoffman [S2]. The pamphlet gives no title or affiliation for him, and no Tier 2 source read for this dossier describes him.

- "Seattle politicians say they care about affordability, then ask residents to approve another regressive sales tax. Proposition 1 would double the Seattle Transit Measure sales tax from 0.15% to 0.30%, putting our sales tax at 10.7%, among the highest rates of any major city in America." [S2]
- "The measure includes funding connected to Sound Transit, even as the agency struggles to deliver projects voters were promised." [S2]
- "At least one-third of riders on Sound Transit boarded without paying during the World Cup. Taxpayers should not continually be asked to pay more while agencies fail to collect fares from riders." [S2] (No source checked for this dossier verifies or refutes the World Cup fare-evasion figure.)
- "Sales taxes disproportionately burden working families and people with lower incomes"; "City Hall should prioritize existing resources, demand responsible management, and improve fare collection before raising taxes." [S2]
- Rebuttal: "The proponents call Proposition 1 'just $29 more,' but that minimizes the real issue: Seattle's sales tax would climb to 10.7% ... Doubling this regressive tax is not affordability." [S2]

Notes on the con statement's claims:

- **10.7%.** This matches the 10.55% current rate plus 0.15% [S13][S21]. With the county roads tax that starts in January 2027, reporters give 10.8% [S22].
- **Sound Transit funding.** The measure's Sound Transit item is City staffing, capped at up to $8 million a year (2.I) [S3]. SDOT plans $5.2-7.1 million a year [S8].
- **Fare collection.** The ordinance's annual reporting section (7.A) requires reporting on fare recovery, "including the percentage of people paying fares" [S3]. Councilmember Rivera added that reporting in committee (Amendment 25, consent package 9-0) [S10][S9]. Councilmember Saka's floor amendment then expanded it on a 5-4 vote, adding a comparison with peer agencies, Metro's actions toward full fare payment, a March 31, 2028 start date and three-year trend comparisons [S6].

## Context / Coverage

**Mayor's proposal.**

- Mayor Katie Wilson announced the proposal on June 2, 2026 [S12][S17][S21]. The Mayor's Office transmitted it to the Council on May 27, 2026 as CB 121226, sponsored by Councilmember Rob Saka [S5].
- **Rate and term.** Wilson's version already set the 0.3% rate and 10-year term that are on the ballot. The Council did not change either [S7][S19].
- **Mayor's spending plan** (Central Staff) [S7]:
  - Metro service $94.6 million
  - streetcar $23.4 million
  - low-income transit access $9.0 million
  - ST3 staffing $6.1 million
  - infrastructure $5 million (raised from $3.5 million in the June 4 presentation)
  - minimum transit-service share of 60%
- **Not in the Mayor's version.** It had no separate transit safety and security line item, no "emerging needs" allowance and no passenger-transportation services category. The 2020 measure had included those [S7].
- **Wilson's case.** She presented transit as "an affordability strategy" [S17][S21]. She said "it's unfortunate that we don't have more progressive options for funding our transit system" [S17][S24].
- **Wilson's background.** She co-founded the Transit Riders Union [S17][S21][S24].

**Council process and amendments.**

- **Timeline.** The Select Committee on Seattle Transportation Benefit District, chaired by Saka, discussed the bill June 18 and July 6, held a public hearing July 13, and recommended it as amended on July 16, 2026 [S5][S12]. The full Council passed it as amended 9-0 on July 21, 2026 [S6][S5]. Mayor Wilson signed it July 30, 2026 [S4][S5].
- **Committee changes adopted** (July 16):
  - *Transit safety and security* (Amendment 1; sponsor Saka, cosponsor Kettle) added Section 2.A.2, recitals about the December 2024 killing of Metro operator Shawn Yim, and a no-supplanting clause for safety services [S10][S3]. It passed 7-2 [S9]. Foster and Rinck voted no [S19][S25]. Foster said safety and security are already "baked into every dollar" the City pays Metro for service [S19]. PubliCola reported that opponents noted $9 million in 2024 transportation levy money for transit security was still unspent [S25].
  - *75% service floor* (Amendment 29, Strauss) raised the minimum transit-service share from the Mayor's 60% to 75% "to reflect the anticipated spending plan as provided by the Executive" [S11]. It passed with eight yes votes and one abstention [S9].
  - *Unspent service funds* (Amendment 3, Saka) added Section 4. It passed 5-4 [S11][S9].
  - *Regional Centers* (Amendment 10, Strauss) and *Teen Late Night service* (Amendment 30, Rivera) each passed 9-0 [S11][S9].
  - *Consent package* passed 9-0 [S9][S10]:
    - electric buses in the Duwamish Valley and South Seattle (Saka/Kettle)
    - midterm evaluation (Foster)
    - progressive-revenue replacement language (Lin; Rinck)
    - night service (Rinck)
    - accessibility (Juarez)
    - Transit Access Program eligibility (Hollingsworth)
    - trade-school students (Rinck)
    - reports on small buses, the Waterfront Shuttle and stop spacing (Kettle)
    - fare-recovery reporting (Rivera)
- **Committee changes rejected** (July 16):
  - *Lower rate* (Amendment 13, Kettle with Rivera) would have cut the rate to 0.225%. City staff said that would raise about $1.04 billion over ten years, $345.3 million less [S11]. It failed 2-7, with only Kettle and Rivera voting yes [S9][S19][S22]. Kettle first proposed 0.2% [S18][S19].
  - *Shorter term* (Amendment 5, Saka) would have cut the term to seven years. It failed 2-7 [S9]. The Seattle Times reports only Kettle joined Saka [S22].
  - *Annual reporting and approval* (Amendment 4, Saka) failed [S9].
  - *Lower capital cap, higher service floor* (Amendment 24, Rinck) would have cut the capital cap to $2 million and raised the floor to 65%. It failed [S11][S9].
- **Floor amendments** (July 21):
  - Hollingsworth's Transit Access Program expansion report passed 9-0 [S6].
  - Rivera's report on routes near schools and community centers passed 7-2, with Lin and Rinck opposed [S6].
  - Saka's amendment to the Section 7 annual report passed 5-4, with Foster, Lin, Rinck and Strauss opposed. It added peer-agency farebox comparisons, Metro's actions toward full fare payment, a March 31, 2028 start date and three-year trend comparisons [S6].
- **Council debate.**
  - Kettle argued for spending "strategically, rather than raising the sales tax simply because we can" [S18].
  - Rivera said the increase "has huge impacts for our low-income folks" [S19].
  - Rinck said the 0.225% rate "would essentially lock us into our current levels of service for the next 10 years" [S19].
  - Saka said "some community priority is going to be left underfunded ... But I would posit to you that it should not be transit" [S19].

**Other context.**

- The Seattle Times reported that 81% of low-income commuters drive or carpool, citing Washington Policy Center analyst Charles Prestrud, who criticized the taxes and the ST3 spending [S21]. It also reported that Metro weekday ridership in March 2026 was about one-third below its 2019 peak [S21].
- The Urbanist and The Seattle Times report the 2020 measure's 80%+ approval [S20][S21].
- The Urbanist, which covered the measure extensively, is itself a listed endorser [S2].
- **Countywide replacement.** The ordinance states the City "supports future efforts to enact a countywide transit funding measure" and could "potentially adjust or phase-out this funding measure" if one passes [S3]. Section 5 provides the mechanism [S3]. Central Staff notes that Metro's long-range plan faces a significant funding gap [S7].

## Axis notes

- `taxes`:
  - A yes vote doubles the dedicated transit sales tax from 0.15% to up to 0.3% for up to ten years [S1][S3]. That is the maximum rate and term the City's transportation benefit district authority allows [S7].
  - Average revenue would be $138 million a year, $1.38 billion in total, against $56.6 million projected in 2026 under the current rate [S8][S7].
  - Seattle's combined rate would go from 10.55% to 10.7%, and to 10.8% with the separate county roads tax [S13][S21][S22].
  - It replaces an expiring tax, but at double the rate. The Council rejected a smaller 0.225% option [S9][S11]. The con statement frames the vote around the regressive sales-tax increase [S2].
- `spending`:
  - A yes vote funds an expansion beyond current service: about 95,000-100,000 more Metro trips or service hours a year [S8][S20], free ORCA passes rising from about 10,000 to 22,000 [S17][S20][S16], and a new transit safety and security category [S3][S10].
  - It also sets a 75% minimum for service spending [S3][S11].
  - The con statement asks the City to "prioritize existing resources" and improve fare collection first [S2].
- `local-control`:
  - Omitted. The measure is a City tax under the City's own transportation benefit district powers and does not change who decides [S3][S7].
  - Section 5 and the recitals do contemplate reducing or phasing out the tax if a countywide King County Transportation District measure or new progressive revenue replaces it [S3]. That is stated intent, not a change in governance.
- Not a measure axis (noted only): the measure allows funding for electric buses in pollution-burdened communities (2.G) [S3]. SDOT's fiscal note says the legislation "will contribute to decreasing Seattle's carbon emissions" [S8].
