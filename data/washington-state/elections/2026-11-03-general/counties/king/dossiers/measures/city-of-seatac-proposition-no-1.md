## What it does

Proposition 1 asks SeaTac voters to raise the city's sales and use tax by "1/10th of 1% (0.1%)." The money would fund "public safety services, including but not limited to, fire protection, fire prevention and emergency medical response; law enforcement support and staffing and retention; criminal justice purposes; public safety operations, maintenance and capital; and other public safety operations and services authorized under RCW 82.14.450" [S1]. The City Council placed it on the ballot with Ordinance No. 26-1017, passed July 14, 2026 [S1][S3].

The city provides public safety services "directly and through contracts with the Puget Sound Regional Fire Authority and King County Sheriff's Office" [S1][S3]. The explanatory statement says Seattle-Tacoma International Airport lies entirely within the city, and visitors produce "a daily visitor population more than six times the City's residential population" [S1]. Revenue would be split 85% to the city and 15% to King County. At least one-third must go "solely for fire protection purposes, criminal justice purposes, or both" [S1][S4].

The ordinance keeps the council's discretion over the money. The council "reserves the right, in accordance with its budget process, to determine the levels of service" funded, and "may alter, make substitutions to, and amend such components" consistent with the ordinance [S3]. One recital says the tax "will provide necessary funding to maintain an essential level of public services such as public safety, transportation, facilities, culture and recreation" [S3].

Passage requires a simple majority (RCW 82.14.450) [S1][S4].

## Cost

- "This tax would add $0.10 to a taxable $100 purchase" [S1].
- The tax applies only to transactions taxable under state law. Most groceries are exempt, and so are motor vehicle sales and the first 36 months of vehicle leases [S1][S4].
- SeaTac's combined sales tax rate is 10.4% in Q4 2026 [S5]. Adding 0.1% would make it 10.5%; that arithmetic was done for this dossier [S5].
- Revenue: the ordinance says the tax "is expected to raise approximately $2 million annually, City will collect $1.7 million (85%)" [S3].
- The explanatory statement and the pro statement say the tax would also be paid by visitors and travelers who make taxable purchases in SeaTac [S1][S2]. The con rebuttal says "The largest transactions at the airport—airline tickets—are exempt from sales tax" [S2]. That claim was not checked for this dossier.

## Fiscal mechanics / What it replaces

- This is a new tax, not a renewal. The ordinance sets no expiration date [S3].
- Budget context from the ordinance recitals [S3]:
  - "approximately 47% of the City's General Fund expenditures are used to provide fire, emergency medical, police and public safety services." The SeaTac Blog put the figure at 46% of the General Fund operating budget [S8].
  - "City property tax revenue growth has not kept pace with inflation since 2009, nor has it kept pace with cost increases in public safety."
  - "beginning in 2027, the City is facing a projected deficit of approximately $7 million per annum."
  - To "close the structural deficit and work toward a $10 million year-over-year budget improvement target, a sales and use tax has been identified as a necessary tool."
- **B&O tax.** In December 2025 the SeaTac Blog reported that the council passed a business and occupation (B&O) tax ordinance, citing the same projected deficit of about $7 million a year and the same $10 million target [S9]. As reported, the B&O tax would be 0.2% for businesses above a $200,000 threshold, expected to raise $2 million to $5 million a year, and expected to take effect January 1, 2027 [S9]. Staff were to spend 2026 on a rate and economic-impact study and on business outreach [S9]. The B&O tax is separate from Proposition 1 and is not on the ballot.
- **County ordering.** RCW 82.14.450 lets counties levy up to 0.3% and cities up to 0.1%. A county's tax is split 60% county / 40% cities (per capita); a city's tax is split 85% city / 15% county [S4]. If a city adopts its ballot ordinance before the county, the county must credit the city's tax against its own to the extent the combined rate would exceed 0.3% [S4].
- If the measure fails, the explanatory statement says "additional resources are needed to continue providing critical fire, medical and public safety services" [S1]. Neither the pamphlet nor the ordinance lists specific cuts [S1][S3].

## Arguments for

From the statement in favor, submitted by James Lovell and Damiana Merryweather [S2]:

- SeaTac is "a unique community. Every day, thousands of workers, travelers, visitors, and airport users come into our city, creating demands on public safety services" [S2].
- "Rather than placing the burden solely on SeaTac residents, this modest 0.1% Public Safety sales tax asks everyone who makes taxable purchases in our community to contribute" [S2].
- "It is only fair that the people who come to SeaTac and rely on our public safety system also contribute to keeping those services strong" [S2].
- "If King County were to impose its own public safety sales tax first, SeaTac would receive a smaller share of that revenue and have less control over its use" [S2]. (The statute's sharing and ordering rules are summarized under Fiscal mechanics [S4].)
- Rebuttal: Proposition 1 "is not a tax on SeaTac residents or local businesses. It is the best tool we have to recover revenue from the thousands of travelers who visit SeaTac every year" [S2].

## Arguments against

From the statement against, submitted by Susan Constable [S2]:

- "The people of SeaTac are awake and watching—again." The statement recounts that in 2014 "the City Council and staff imposed a utility tax," and in 2015 "voters replaced that council, the utility tax was repealed, and the City Manager fired." It says the new council "even rejected the automatic property-tax increase" and that this "began ten years of fiscal accountability, new sidewalks, surplus funds, and increased police staffing" [S2].
- "The surplus is gone, spending out of control, and now they want to raise taxes again—this time on our local businesses" [S2].
- "The B&O tax is a red flag for what could be coming if we don't change course" [S2].
- Rebuttal: "A Public Safety Sales Tax is even higher than a B&O tax. Those, and new utility taxes, were all planned for at the same council meeting." "The Airport is a red herring." "These new taxes are regressive and hurt residents" [S2].

**What sources confirm about the 2014-2015 history:**
- On October 28, 2014 the council approved a 6% utility tax, 5 to 2, to balance the 2015-2016 budget. Councilmembers Pam Fernald and Terry Anderson voted no [S10].
- After the November 2015 election, the outgoing council majority repealed the tax at its final meeting on December 8, 2015, effective March 1. Councilmember Barry Ladenburg, who had been defeated in that election, moved the repeal [S11]. The new members taking office in January 2016 were Rick Forschler, Peter Kwon, Erin Sitterley and Michael Siefkes [S11].
- These sources do not show who repealed the tax in the way the statement describes ("voters replaced that council, the utility tax was repealed"). The vote was taken by the outgoing council, not the new one [S11].
- No source found confirms the statement's claims about the City Manager being fired, the rejected property-tax increase, or ten years of surpluses. Those claims are the statement's own.
- No source found confirms the rebuttal's claim that new utility taxes were planned at the same meeting as this measure and the B&O tax.

## Context / Coverage

- **Council vote.** The SeaTac Blog reported that the council voted unanimously on July 14, 2026 to send the measure to the ballot [S8]. It also reported these remarks [S8]:
  - Councilmember Peter Kwon said it was in the city's interest to act because if King County imposed the tax first, the revenue split would "disproportionately benefit the county."
  - Councilmember James Lovell said: "I don't think anyone here wants to see a tax that hits our residents first ... We need a tool. And this tool is available to us."
- Peter Kwon was one of the councilmembers elected in the 2015 election that followed the utility-tax dispute [S11].
- The ordinance's 2026 council priorities include "achieve and maintain fiscal sustainability," "fund public safety," and "obtain shared revenue models that recognize the fiscal impacts of hosting the SeaTac International Airport" [S3].
- **Coverage.** Coverage came only from The SeaTac Blog, a hyperlocal outlet that is the B-Town Blog's sister site [S8][S9]. No coverage was found in the Seattle Times, KING5 or Westside Seattle. The city's website (seatacwa.gov) refused automated requests (HTTP 403), so council agendas and staff reports could not be reviewed. The ordinance text came from King County Elections [S3].

## Axis notes

- `taxes`: a yes vote adds a new 0.1% sales and use tax with no expiration date, expected to raise about $2 million a year [S1][S3]. The city has also passed a separate B&O tax due in 2027 [S9]. The con case is mainly about taxes [S2].
- `spending`: the ordinance and explanatory statement describe sustaining public safety services and closing a structural deficit, not expanding programs [S1][S3]. Section 1 of the ordinance allows hiring "additional personnel" "if revenues are sufficient" [S3].
- `local-control`: the measure does not change who governs or delivers services; the fire and police contracts stay in place [S1][S3]. The pro statement's "local control" argument is about the city's share of tax revenue compared with a possible county tax [S2][S4].
- `social` / `parental-rights`: not addressed [S1][S2].
