## What it does

Proposition 2 is a four-year capital levy for the district's Capital Projects Fund. It would collect $12,000,000 a year in 2027, 2028, 2029 and 2030 [S1][S3]. The money would go to districtwide technology and to other capital improvements "not funded by the State" [S1]. The ballot title lists cybersecurity protections, reliable internet, technology operations and infrastructure, and student/staff technology access [S1].

Resolution No. 12-26, adopted July 1, 2026, describes the projects more broadly [S3]:

- Districtwide technology "programs, staff, computers, personal computing devices, hardware and software."
- Cybersecurity and internet.
- Student and staff training.
- Ongoing fees for online applications, subscriptions and software licenses.
- "Districtwide capital acquisitions and improvements to support the construction, modernization and remodeling of schools and facilities."

The district's anticipated breakdown of the 2027 levy year ($12 million) [S5]:

| Category | 2027 amount |
|---|---|
| Professional learning and training for staff | $1.5M |
| Technology infrastructure and cybersecurity | $1.7M |
| Classroom technology | $0.5M |
| Student devices | $2.5M |
| Teacher devices | $1.0M |
| Instructional and operational systems | $2.8M |
| Capital improvements | $2.0M |

The district's page lists building automation and HVAC controls, electrical infrastructure, fire alarm and life-safety technology, security and access control, and intercom and low-voltage systems as facility items the levy would fund [S5].

Passage requires a simple majority [S1].

## Cost

- Ballot title (verbatim): estimated levy rate per $1,000 of assessed value of "$0.36" (2027), "$0.35" (2028), "$0.34" (2029) and "$0.32" (2030), with "$12,000,000" each year [S1].
- The district gives no household figure for this levy alone. Its combined example for Prop. 1 and Prop. 2 together: the total school rate rises from $4.47 to $4.89 per $1,000, "$210 annually, or $17.50 per month" on a $500,000 home [S6][S7].
- Total over four years: $48 million [S6].

## Fiscal mechanics / What it replaces

- **The earlier levy.** Highline's earlier technology levy was a two-year, $32.5 million levy approved in November 2020. It was collected at an estimated 67 cents per $1,000 in 2021 and 2022 [S8], and it passed with 73.8% in early returns [S9]. The district's tech levy page says: "Highline voters approved a two-year technology levy in 2020. The 2026 technology levy resumes that funding" [S5].
- **A new levy after a gap.** No technology levy was collected in 2023 through 2026, as far as the sources found show [S5][S8]. Prop. 2 is a new levy after a four-year gap, not a back-to-back renewal. The district describes it as resuming local technology funding [S4][S5][S6].
- **Size compared with 2020.** The proposed $12 million a year at $0.32-$0.36 is less per year than the 2020 levy, which raised $32.5 million over two years at 67 cents [S1][S8].
- **"Restores expired funding."** The pro statement says the levy "restores expired funding approved by voters." The pro rebuttal calls the 2020 levy "Highline's one/only tech levy" [S2]. The con statement says "Voters have approved multiple tech levies since 2018" [S2]. District materials reviewed mention only the 2020 technology levy [S5]. Highline's separate education (EP&O) levy, renewed in November 2025 with 60.31%, lists "classroom technology, software and cybersecurity systems" among its uses [S10][S11].
- **If money is left over or projects change.** Section 5 of the resolution says that if the taxes exceed project costs, or "state or local circumstances require any alteration in the Projects," the district "may apply such taxes to other capital purposes, or reduce or eliminate the excess property tax levies," "as the Board may determine." If projects become impractical, the district "will not be required to accomplish such Projects" [S3]. The opposition committee cites section 5 as letting the district reallocate funds at its discretion [S15].
- **If it fails.** The district does not say what happens [S4][S5][S6].

## Policy 6101 dispute

- **Con statement:** "with Policy 6101 just changed in June, it will give administrators upfront authority to sign million-dollar tech contracts without a public meeting or school board vote" [S2]. The committee's site says the updated policy gives "administrators upfront authorization to execute major contracts up to $1,000,000 without requiring individual public School Board votes" [S15].
- **Pro rebuttal:** "Fact check: Policy 6101 still requires school board approval of million-dollar tech contracts" [S2].
- **What the policy says.** Policy 6101 (Donations, Grants, and Contracts) was adopted in June 2026 ("6.26"), effective August 1, 2026. It replaced former policies 6101 Grants, 6114 Donations and 6225 Contracts [S12]. Its contract thresholds [S12]:
  - **General fund (non-capital):** board approval for contracts of $500,000 or more and amendments of $250,000 or more.
  - **Capital, non-bond/levy projects:** board approval for contracts of $1,000,000 or more.
  - **Capital, bond/levy projects:** "School Board approval of the projects named within a levy or bond proposal constitutes the initial acceptance of all contractual commitments." After that, the board must approve only change orders or amendments "valued at more than $1,000,000" and any project that "exceeds the total initial budgeted amount."
- **Procedure 6101 P3** covers non-construction contracts. It sets approval chains only for general-fund contracts, with board approval over $500,000 [S13].
- **Which category applies?** Neither document says which category covers technology purchases paid from this capital levy [S12][S13]. The opposition's reading treats them as "bond/levy projects." The rebuttal's reading is consistent with the general-fund and non-bond/levy capital thresholds. The district's prior thresholds were not found, so the opposition committee's claim that the change raised the superintendent's authority from $250,000 is unverified [S14][S15].

## Arguments for

From the statement in favor, submitted by Trisha Arias, Karen Keiser, Pat Thompson, www.yesforHighline.org [S2]:

- "Voting yes on Prop. 2 restores expired funding approved by voters for the secure systems, reliable internet, and classroom tools that prepare students for success" [S2].
- It "funds cybersecurity to protect sensitive student information," software, internet, teacher training, technical support and devices, and "the system that enables schools to reach parents in an emergency" [S2].
- "Technology costs are rising. The levy fills the gap between the funding provided by the state and the actual costs" [S2].
- Rebuttal: "This levy funds technology for student safety, parent communication, and cybersecurity - not more screentime" [S2]. It repeats the Policy 6101 point and the "one/only tech levy in 2020" claim [S2].
- The committee's website: "Technology doesn't replace teachers," and "The vast majority of classroom time is spent in direct, teacher-led instruction" [S16].

## Arguments against

From the statement against, submitted by Mike Bishoff, Katie Paulson, Lauren Schmidt, BetterPlanforHighline.com [S2]:

- "Real student success comes from dedicated teachers, not screen time"; "our children and teachers deserve proven, human-led instruction over endless tech contracts" [S2].
- "Highline's 1:1 device policy gives every child an internet-connected device starting in Kindergarten"; "We need to pause and reassess how tech is being used in our classrooms" [S2].
- "Voters have approved multiple tech levies since 2018, yet academic outcomes have gone down"; it calls i-Ready "expensive and unproven" [S2].
- "This levy is a blank check," citing Policy 6101 (see above) [S2].
- Rebuttal: "Vote No to demand district accountability: audit all tech contracts, remove 1:1 devices in early grades, establish clear districtwide screen policies, and restore direct school board oversight over academics and spending" [S2].
- The committee's website adds three more objections [S15]:
  - Third-party software raises student data-privacy concerns.
  - Packaging "routine facility upkeep (such as painting, flooring, and paving)" into a "technology" levy misleads voters.
  - The 2020 levy was $32.5 million.

## Axis notes

- `taxes`: a yes vote adds a levy of $0.32-$0.36 per $1,000 for 2027-2030 [S1]. The levy is a resumption after a lapse since 2022, at about half the 2020 levy's rate [S5][S8]. For current bills it is a new line item; the district projects the combined increase with Prop. 1 at $0.42 per $1,000 [S7].
- `spending`: the con case is framed as accountability (audit tech contracts, board oversight) and doubts about educational technology [S2]. The pro case frames the levy as restoring funding for existing systems, not new programs [S2][S5].
- `parental-rights`: the con case asks to remove 1:1 devices in early grades and adopt districtwide screen policies [S2]. Its website raises student data privacy [S15]. These are demands for district-wide policy and board oversight. Neither statement asks for parental notification, record access or opt-out rights over devices or software [S2][S15], so the dispute does not sit on the rubric's notification/opt-out axis.
- `local-control` / `social`: not addressed [S1][S2].
