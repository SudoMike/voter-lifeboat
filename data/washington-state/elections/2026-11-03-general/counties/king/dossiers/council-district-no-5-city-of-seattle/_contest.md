## What the office does

A Seattle City Councilmember "approves the city's budget, develops laws and policies, and oversees public services"; the 2026 salary is $166,058, and this seat is on the ballot for an unexpired one-year term [C1]. The council has nine members, seven elected by district. It sets land-use and zoning policy, levies city taxes, and oversees SPD, transportation and housing programs [C2]. District 5 is North Seattle, including Lake City, Aurora and Northgate/Pinehurst [C2].

## Race dynamics

This special election fills the remainder of the term vacated when Councilmember Cathy Moore resigned in July 2025. The council appointed Debora Juarez to hold the seat until the 2026 results are certified [C2]. Neither general-election candidate is an incumbent, and both were finalists for that appointment [C2]. Nilu Jenks led the August primary with 53.95%, ahead of Julie Kang at 32.98% (Dimitri Georgakopoulos 8.03%, Silas James 4.81%) [C3]. As of early October, Jenks had raised $180,463 and Kang $151,669 [C7][C8].

Both candidates identify as Democrats and oppose state initiatives I-001 and I-638 [C1][C9]. The general-election debate has centered on public safety along Aurora Avenue [C4][C5]. Kang supports SOAP orders and public-safety cameras, after changing her earlier position on cameras. Jenks opposes both and emphasizes alternative response teams, though she also supports a 1,200-officer target and a supplementary North Seattle precinct [C4][C5][C6]. On revenue, Jenks favors progressive revenue, including a data-center tax, while Kang opposes new taxes on residents and stresses return on investment [C5][C6]. Jenks's endorsements include King County Democrats, Working Families Party and multiple unions, and FOX 13 reports Mayor Wilson's support. Kang's include the Seattle Times (primary), the 32nd LD Democrats and Councilmembers Kettle and Rivera [C1][C2][C5].

## Issue axes that genuinely differentiate these candidates

1. **Safety (enforcement vs. prevention):** Kang: SPD staffing, a second precinct, SOAP, cameras, shutting open-air drug markets. Jenks: CARE Department, community court and violence intervention, opposition to SOAP and camera expansion. Both support some police staffing [C4][C5][C6].
2. **Taxes:** Jenks favors progressive revenue and proposed an AI data-center tax. Kang opposes new taxes on residents, criticizes "another regressive sales tax hike," and voiced sales-tax concerns about the transit measure [C5][C6].
3. **Housing:** both support more housing near transit. Jenks pairs a 1,000-unit affordable-housing target with tenant protections. Kang emphasizes permitting reform and replacing MHA fees with JumpStart revenue. The difference is mostly in method [C1][C2][C6].
4. **Reform:** Jenks works at FairVote and prioritizes implementing ranked-choice voting in 2027. Kang has no comparable plank [C1].

Experience does not strongly separate them: neither has held elected office. Jenks is an advocacy and political professional; Kang is an educator and bank co-founder [C1][C2].
