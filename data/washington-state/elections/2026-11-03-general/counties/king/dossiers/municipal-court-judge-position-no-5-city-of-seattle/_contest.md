## What the office does

Seattle Municipal Court judges hear and decide misdemeanor cases for the City of Seattle; the 2026 salary is $214,791 and the term is four years [S1]. The court handles misdemeanors carrying up to 364 days in jail, such as DUI, domestic violence, theft and trespass, and its seven elected judges and five magistrates also hear civil infractions [S6]. Day-to-day judicial discretion in this court centers on pretrial release, diversion and specialty calendars such as Veterans Treatment Court, which the retiring incumbent runs [S3][S2].

## Race dynamics

- Open seat. Incumbent Judge Willie Gregory, first elected in 2011, has a term running January 2023 to January 2027 and presides over Veterans Treatment Court [S3]. Newsom's general-election statement lists him as "retiring Judge Willie Gregory" and as an endorser [S1]. The court's own page does not state that he is retiring [S3].
- Primary result (Aug. 4, 2026): Gabe Rothstein 105,878 votes (54.6%), Garmon Newsom 53,988 (27.9%), Lindsay Calkins 33,291 (17.2%), write-ins 668 (0.3%) [S4]. Calkins had announced her withdrawal before the primary but remained on the ballot [S2].
- Professional split: Rothstein is a public defender since 2005 and a judge pro tem since 2024 [S1][S10]. Newsom is a former prosecutor and city attorney (Bronx DA, Renton, Burien) and a Seattle Municipal Court pro tem judge [S1][S6].
- Bar rating: the King County Bar Association rated Rothstein "Well Qualified" (July 14, 2026) and lists Newsom as N/A, meaning not rated within three years [S5].
- Endorsement blocs: the Seattle Times editorial board recommended Newsom in the primary [S6]. The Stranger endorsed Rothstein [S7]. Rothstein lists the King County Democrats and several legislative-district Democratic organizations [S1].
- Fundraising (PDC, as of early October 2026): Newsom about $115,600 raised, including $27,500 of his own funds, and about $70,700 spent; Rothstein about $39,200 raised and $17,200 spent [S8][S9].

## What genuinely differentiates the candidates

- **Professional formation (`safety`).** Rothstein comes from a 20-year public-defense career [S1]. Newsom comes from prosecution and municipal-government law [S1][S6]. The Stranger reported that, at a January 2025 Burien City Council meeting, Newsom said threatening homeless people with arrest would give them an "incentive to actually accept the offer of shelter, treatment, and housing" [S7].
- **Documented release posture (`safety`).** Rothstein's site says release decisions should turn on whether a person will return to court and whether they pose a danger, "not whether they have sufficient resources to afford bail" [S10]. Newsom's materials frame diversion as part of "protecting the community" and contain no comparable bail statement [S1].
- **Judicial philosophy (`judicial`).** Newsom says judges should not be "focused on politics and ideology," and told the Seattle Times that Seattle does not need "a prosecutor or a public defender... on the bench" [S1][S6]. Rothstein frames the court as "the people's court" and stresses treatment, recovery and second chances [S1].
- **Experience (`experience`).** Newsom reports 34 years of practice, including King County District Court pro tem service in 2013–2015 and current Seattle Municipal Court pro tem service [S1][S6]. Rothstein reports 70+ trials and pro tem service since 2024 in six courts [S1][S10]. Only Rothstein has a published KCBA rating [S5].

## Not differentiating

- Both say they will treat every person in court with dignity, and both support treatment and diversion in some form [S1].
- Both self-report "Exceptionally Well Qualified" ratings from overlapping minority and affinity bar associations. Newsom's statement that he is "the only candidate" with the highest rating conflicts with Rothstein's site, which claims the same ratings [S1][S10].
- Party endorsements are recorded as facts only. This is a nonpartisan judicial office, so they are not evidence of judicial philosophy.
