## What the office does

A Metropolitan King County Councilmember "makes laws and adopts county budget," is elected by district to a four-year term, and is paid $187,547 (2026) [S1]. The nine-member council sets the county budget and county levies to put before voters, and oversees Metro Transit, public health, the jail and the Sheriff's Office, regional homelessness funding, and land use in unincorporated areas [S2]. District 2 covers the University District, Laurelhurst, Ravenna, Eastlake, Capitol Hill, the Central District, South Seattle, Allentown and Skyway [S4].

## Race dynamics

The seat is open. Girmay Zahilay left it to become County Executive in November 2025, and the council appointed Rhonda Lewis as interim member until the November result is certified [S4]. State Sen. Rebecca Saldaña (37th LD since 2016) led the August 4 primary with 55.8%; Port of Seattle Commissioner Toshiko Grace Hasegawa took 35.0% and Miriam Mboya 8.7% [S3].

Both finalists are progressive Democrats with overlapping endorsements (Councilmember Mosqueda and several Seattle councilmembers and legislators back both). Saldaña has the King County Democrats' sole endorsement, service-sector and public-employee unions, and The Urbanist's and The Stranger's primary endorsements. Hasegawa has Executive Zahilay, former Governors Inslee and Locke, building-trades and maritime unions, and the Seattle Times' primary endorsement. Each has raised roughly a quarter-million dollars (candidate dossiers). Northwest Asian Weekly's report on an October 3 debate found the two gave similar answers and "supported the same progressive measures" [S5].

## Issue axes that genuinely differentiate these candidates

1. **Kind of experience (`experience`).** Saldaña brings ten years of legislative work and a committee chair, including sponsorship of and votes for the 2026 tax on income over $1 million. Hasegawa brings executive and port-district experience (state commission director, Port commissioner and 2025 commission president). Both are current elected officials, so neither is an outsider.
2. **County revenue tools (`taxes`).** Both favor taxing high earners. Hasegawa's platform is specific about county tools: lobbying to lift the 1% property-tax cap, a countywide housing levy, high-earner excise taxes and a public bank. Saldaña's record is at the state level (capital gains and income taxes on the wealthy); in June she was more hesitant about a county property-tax levy for social housing.
3. **Housing (`housing`).** Hasegawa details social housing at scale, land banking near transit, a "Stay Housed Guarantee" and right-to-return; Saldaña emphasizes housing "affordable at any income," Housing First and anti-displacement programs.
4. **Public safety (`safety`).** Both lean toward prevention and services. Hasegawa's platform is more detailed (LEAD expansion, zero youth incarceration goal, ending pretextual stops, OLEO subpoena power); Saldaña cites community-based prevention, behavioral health and jail-standards work.

Axes with little difference: `spending` (both propose expanded county programs, including universal or subsidized child care), `climate` (both cite climate work; Saldaña has a legislative climate record), and `reform`. On the statewide initiatives, Saldaña voted for the income tax I-645 would repeal and, per a July forum report, said she would push to defeat initiatives undercutting a wealth tax; Hasegawa has no stated position on any of the three.
