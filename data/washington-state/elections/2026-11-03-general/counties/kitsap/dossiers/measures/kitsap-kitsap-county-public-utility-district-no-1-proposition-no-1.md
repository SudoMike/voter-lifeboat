## Ballot title (verbatim)

> Kitsap County Public Utility District No. 1
> Proposition No. 1
> Authorizing the Construction or Acquisition of Electric Facilities
> Shall Public Utility District No. 1 of Kitsap County construct or acquire electric facilities for the generation, transmission or distribution of electric power?

[S1][S4]. The wording is fixed by state law, RCW 54.08.070 [S9][S3].

## Explanatory statement (verbatim, prepared by the attorney for the district)

> Proposition 1 asks voters to authorize Kitsap County Public Utility District No. 1 ("KPUD") to provide electric services within Kitsap County. Under Washington law, KPUD cannot provide these services without first obtaining voter approval. KPUD was formed in 1940 and currently provides water, telecommunications, and waste-water services to over 45,000 residents throughout the county. KPUD is governed by a three-member board of elected officials, one for each district of the county. While this proposal would give KPUD authority to provide electric services, it does not require or guarantee that KPUD will do so. If this measure passes, KPUD's Board has agreed to conduct a feasibility and investigation period to first determine if it is in the community's best interest before constructing or acquiring electric facilities, or providing electric service.

[S1]

## What it does

A yes vote gives KPUD standing legal authority to build or buy electric generation, transmission or distribution facilities. A PUD that did not get into the electric business within ten years of its creation needs voter approval first, and a simple majority grants it [S9]. KPUD was formed in 1940 to provide electric service, but World War II intervened and it never acquired an electric system [S3][S5]. Today it runs drinking water systems (more than 35,000 customers), wastewater plants at Port Gamble and Bainbridge Island, an open-access fiber network and countywide water-resource monitoring [S3]. Puget Sound Energy (PSE) provides electric service in most of Kitsap County [S7][S8].

The whole county votes. The explanatory statement describes service "within Kitsap County" and a board with one commissioner "for each district of the county" [S1], and KPUD and the news coverage describe the question as put to "Kitsap County voters" [S2][S8].

**What a yes vote does not do**, per KPUD: it does not change today's provider, set rates, transfer ownership of existing facilities, approve financing or bonds, require KPUD to acquire a system or provide service, or set a timeline [S2][S3].

**What happens if it passes.** On July 28, 2026 the board voted to place the measure on the ballot and passed a motion that, if it passes, a feasibility study will be done before KPUD takes any action to use the authority [S2]. KPUD says the study would look at infrastructure, power supply, costs and financing, rates, staffing and legal requirements, and its results would go to the board in public [S3]. Any decision to pursue electric service would then be made by the board at a public meeting [S3]. The statute does not require a second public vote. A majority yes "authorize[s]" the district to construct or acquire electric facilities [S9].

**What happens if it fails.** KPUD would not get the authority and the study would not go forward. Any future attempt would need a new process [S3][S8], for example a citizen petition, which could next reach the ballot in November 2028 [S5].

**Why the board asked for authority before a study.** The board says residents asked it to look at electric costs [S6][S7]. A comprehensive study would cost an estimated $300,000-$500,000 [S3][S6]. The general manager's June memo put it at $300,000-$600,000 [S5]. Of four such measures statewide in 2008 and 2012, one passed, so the board chose to ask voters first [S3][S6]. KPUD's own presentation says plainly that the measure "is for authority to construct or acquire electric facilities" and "is not to authorize a feasibility study" [S6].

**Acquisition by condemnation.** If PSE will not sell, KPUD "could consider pursuing a court process to acquire the system" under RCW 54.16.020, with the court determining fair value [S3][S5].

**Rates and regulation.** A PUD sets its own electric rates "free from the jurisdiction and control of the utilities and transportation commission" [S10]. PSE is an investor-owned utility regulated by the state Utilities and Transportation Commission [S3].

## Cost

- The ballot title names no tax, rate or dollar amount [S1].
- KPUD: "Proposition 1 would not immediately change property taxes or electric rates." It "does not authorize a property-tax increase or constitute a levy lid lift." KPUD's current levy is about $0.045 per $1,000, about $22.50 a year on a $500,000 home [S3].
- Costs that follow from a yes vote by board decision, not by the ballot title: the feasibility study ($300,000-$500,000) [S3][S6].
- KPUD pays for this election because the board, not a petition, placed it on the ballot. The general manager estimated $50,000-$100,000 [S5].
- Financing capacity KPUD already has under state law: general obligation bonds up to 1.25% of assessed value (about $819.6 million in 2026), revenue bonds beyond that, and a regular levy of up to $0.45 per $1,000 without a vote. Any increase above 1% a year needs voter approval [S5]. The measure does not approve any bonds [S3].
- Comparison point: Jefferson PUD, the last Washington PUD to win electric authority (2008, 53.3% yes, by citizen petition), bought PSE's Jefferson County system for $103 million in 2010 and began operating it in April 2013 [S5]. PSE puts the Jefferson cost at $109 million [S8]. The memo notes that Kitsap's population is about ten times Jefferson's in 2010 [S5].
- No cost estimate for acquiring PSE's Kitsap system was found in KPUD or news sources read for this dossier.

## Arguments for

From the statement in favor, by David Behar, William Woznicki and Sue Ellen White, "Committee for the Measure" [S1]:

- A yes vote "enables Kitsap Public Utility District to conduct a transparent, state-authorized feasibility study of local public power... If the numbers do not work, the effort stops."
- Lower-cost power: "Washington has 23 electric PUDs, and 2025 rate data show their residential energy rates substantially below PSE's." The study could show whether Bonneville power and the public model give "more stable, affordable rates." KPUD's fact publication counts 24 of Washington's 28 PUDs as providing electric service [S3].
- Local value: "In 2024, PSE paid shareholders $175.9 million and continues to raise our rates. Let's keep our money!" This figure was not checked against PSE filings for this dossier.
- Local accountability: decisions would sit "closer to the people who pay the bills."
- Rebuttal (to the statement against): "This measure does not obligate KPUD to buy anything... Any future acquisition would still require cost studies, financing, public review, and open decision-making... Vote yes to keep responsible options available."

## Arguments against

From the statement against, by Varon Mullis (chair), Robert P. McGee and Shannon Turner, "Citizens for Accountable Utilities in Kitsap" [S1]:

- "This measure grants KPUD broad, permanent authority to build or acquire an electric utility, before providing the financial evidence to justify it." It cites Jefferson County, where the PUD "went ahead anyway, without a second vote."
- It cites a projected regional electricity shortfall growing "from roughly 1 gigawatt today to 9 gigawatts by 2030" and retail rates that "could rise up to 47% above inflation regardless of who provides Kitsap's power." No source is named in the statement for these figures.
- "Voters in Skagit, Island, and Thurston counties have already rejected similar measures." KPUD's memo confirms the Skagit and Island 2008 failures and the Thurston 2012 failure [S5]. The statement says Whatcom PUD and the Bainbridge Island City Council studied the idea and chose not to proceed. That claim was not verified for this dossier.
- KPUD "doesn't need this vote to begin a cost-benefit study; it has that authority now."
- "A 'No' vote does not kill KPUD's power plans. It isn't a vote for PSE. It's a vote for transparency and informed consent before expanding government authority."
- Rebuttal (to the statement for): "This measure does not authorize a study. It grants permanent authority to construct or acquire electric facilities, with no second vote required." It lists PSE's local spending since 2020, including "$200m for reliability," "$8m in taxes paid annually to local governments" and 70 Kitsap employees.

Note on the VoteWA record: the field labeled "RebuttalFor" holds the against committee's rebuttal of the statement for, and "RebuttalAgainst" holds the for committee's rebuttal [S1]. They are attributed above by content.

## Positions of the utilities

- **KPUD board.** Before the vote, the board said it had "not taken a position either in favor of or opposed to pursuing electric service" [S7]. Board President Debra Lester called the July 28 action "one step in a much longer process, and the voters will determine whether that process continues" [S8]. All three commissioners signed Resolution 26-07-28A: Lester (President), Heather Pauley (Vice-President) and Stacey Smith (Secretary) [S4].
- **PSE.** A July 28 PSE release, as reported by the Kitsap Daily News, said KPUD is "asking voters for a blank check." It said KPUD has no public plan for serving "more than 130,000 customers" and that yes would let "the three commissioners... proceed with full acquisition of PSE's system regardless of the cost" with "no requirement for another public vote" [S8]. The article also reports PSE saying its power supply costs have more than tripled since the state passed the Climate Commitment Act and now run about $2 billion a year [S8]. These are PSE's figures and were not checked.

## Lean notes

- `taxes`: no mapping. The measure levies no tax and changes no rate [S1][S3]. KPUD's existing levy and bonding authority do not depend on this vote [S5]. Any later financing is a separate board decision [S3].
- `spending`: no mapping. A yes vote authorizes no spending. The board's own motion commits it to a $300,000-$500,000 study if it passes [S2][S3], and a later acquisition could be large [S5][S8]. Both sides frame their position as "facts first": the pro side wants a study before any decision, and the con side wants cost estimates before granting authority [S1]. Neither framing places a yes vote on the audit-versus-invest axis without inferring what the board would later do.
- `local-control`: no mapping. One reading is that a yes vote moves electric rate-setting from the state UTC to a locally elected board [S10][S3], and the pro statement argues "local accountability" [S1]. But the switch is from a private, state-regulated utility to a countywide public one. That is public versus private ownership, not a choice between a smaller local unit and a regional or state standard. The rubric's poles do not cleanly fit it.
- `social`, `parental-rights`: not addressed.
