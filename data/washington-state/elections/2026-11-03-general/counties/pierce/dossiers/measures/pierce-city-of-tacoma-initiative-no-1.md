## What it does

Initiative Measure No. 1, the "Safe Homes for All" initiative, is a citizen initiative that would amend Tacoma's rental housing code and business license code [S1]. Per the ballot title, it would improve compliance with tenant safety and protection laws "through: landlord education and training, recognizing tenants' right to organize, requiring landlords to bargain in good faith with tenant unions, establishing a public landlord information database, providing for City and private enforcement of tenant safety and protection laws, imposition of penalties and cost recovery, requiring landlord rental licensing and fees, authorizing revocation of landlord business licenses, prohibiting retaliation, and establishing certain exemptions" [S1].

The explanatory statement adds that the measure would make certain provisions of state landlord-tenant law enforceable under the city code. It would let tenants file administrative complaints "requiring investigation and enforcement by the City," and require inspections for habitability or safety violations [S1]. It would transfer penalties to the affected tenants and require the city to reimburse tenants' and their organizations' costs and attorney fees when they successfully enforce or defend the initiative [S1].

Main provisions in the initiative text posted by the sponsor [S5]:

- **Tenant unions.** A tenant union is recognized once it shows support from at least one resident in more than 50% of occupied units in a building or complex. Recognition lasts three years. Landlords must then "engage in good faith bargaining" on conditions, building policies, lease agreements, fees, deposits, pets and utilities, and attend at least one union meeting per quarter on request. Landlords must post a notice of the right to form a tenant union [S5].
- **Enforcement.** Any tenant, tenant union or tenant advocate may file a complaint with the city or sue in Superior Court [S5]. Penalties are "not less than $500 and up to five times the monthly rent" per violation and go to the affected tenants. A penalty for a state-law habitability violation may be waived if the landlord fixes it promptly and the tenant suffered no undue hardship [S5].
- **Repeat offenders.** A "large landlord" controls 25 or more units, counting affiliated companies inside and outside Tacoma. A "repeat offender" has six or more violations in 36 months or a pattern of violations [S5]. Large for-profit landlords who are repeat offenders pay an extra penalty covering the city's enforcement costs and lose eligibility for exemptions from Tacoma tenant laws [S5].
- **Small landlords and nonprofits.** "Small landlords" (4 or fewer units) are exempt from the bargaining and notice requirements. Small landlords and nonprofit and public housing providers are exempt from the cost-recovery penalty [S5]. Nonprofit low-income housing providers get a separate fee structure and a longer phase-in [S5].
- **Rental licensing.** The city must create a per-unit rental license fee "not to exceed the reasonable costs" of enforcing tenant laws. Large landlords and repeat offenders must be licensed within 18 months and pay higher fees. All other landlords must be licensed within three years [S5].
- **Other.** A public database of landlords, their units, evictions and rent increases is due within 18 months [S5]. The city may revoke a business license for violations posing "a serious threat of loss of life or serious bodily injury" [S5]. Retaliation against tenants for exercising these rights is prohibited [S5].

Under the City Charter, a majority vote enacts an initiated ordinance, which takes effect ten days after the election is certified unless it sets another date [S9].

## Cost

- The measure creates no tax. It requires a per-unit rental license fee capped at the city's reasonable costs of regulation [S1][S5].
- **Opponents' figures:** the statement against says "The City's estimated annual cost to do this is $8 to $10 million," and that recovering it "will require an average annual per unit licensing fee of $191" [S1]. It says the fee delay of "18 months to 3 years" will "worsen the City's $40M budget shortfall" [S1].
- **Supporters' figures:** the pro rebuttal cites The News Tribune as reporting that "the City has not published any studies" on the cost [S1]. It says similar programs in Washington run on "$10 to $35 per-unit landlord fees" [S1]. Tacoma For All's FAQ says the $10 million figure came from "a brief verbal exchange" by the City Manager at a council meeting. Using $15 to $35 a unit across about 45,000 rental units, it estimates $700,000 to $1.5 million a year [S3].
- This research did not find a published City of Tacoma fiscal estimate. The News Tribune article quoted in the rebuttal could not be read.

## Fiscal mechanics / What it replaces

- **Existing law.** In 2023 Tacoma voters passed the Landlord Fairness Code Initiative, which the sponsors call the "Tenant Bill of Rights" [S2][S8]. On December 9, 2025 the City Council amended it with Ordinance 29086, effective January 1, 2026. The council cited costs to low-income housing providers and exempted landlords with four or fewer units from the cold-weather eviction ban. It also required relocation assistance on request for rent increases over 5% in 12 months [S8].
- **Enforcement record.** Tacoma For All says that since 2023 "Just one fine against a law-breaking landlord has been issued," and that the council has favored voluntary compliance over penalties [S2][S3]. This claim was not checked against city records.
- **Existing licensing.** The statement against says Tacoma already requires landlords who violate their duties to obtain a "provisional license" with fees of up to $2,000 [S1].
- **Signatures.** Tacoma For All says nearly 9,000 voters signed petitions [S3].

## Arguments for

From the statement in favor, by committee members Tyron Moore, Kiss'Shonna Curtis and Bill Hanawalt (TacomaForAll.info) [S1]:

- "We cannot allow outside real estate investors to threaten housing affordability with illegal rent hikes or force families to live with hazardous black mold. Measure 1 establishes basic enforcement and protects renters from retaliation when they organize and speak out" [S1].
- "Most Tacoma tenants rent from out-of-town landlords prioritizing profit over safety and affordability. Measure 1 protects affordable housing, while offering resources to small landlords" [S1].
- "Enforcement should be funded by repeat violators and corporate landlords exploiting our neighbors—not taxpayers" [S1].
- "Outside real estate interests spent nearly $400,000 trying to buy Tacoma's 2023 election and block tenant protections" [S1].
- Rebuttal: the cost claims are "wild"; "Measure 1 allows quick fee collection, protecting taxpayers and city services. Renter rights mean nothing without enforcement against slumlords" [S1].

## Arguments against

From the statement against, by committee members Michael Mirra, Ben Lackey and Mark Melsness [S1]:

- "This proposal makes housing, including affordable housing, harder to develop and more expensive. Rents will go up" [S1].
- "It directs the City to create a new bureaucracy to license rental units and investigate and adjudicate tenant complaints," at an estimated $8 to $10 million a year. It says the delayed fee collection will worsen the city's $40 million budget shortfall, "deepening cuts to fire fighters, public safety, and housing" [S1].
- "Fees will increase rents," at an average of $191 per unit per year [S1].
- "Tacoma already has some of the strongest renter protections in the country" [S1].
- Rebuttal: "The proposal dodges" the city's annual budget process and "redirects funds from other public services, including affordable housing, while not adding a square inch of housing in Tacoma" [S1].

Sean Flynn, president of the Rental Housing Association of Washington, told KNKX that tenant unions do not fit rental housing because renters can move and negotiate individually, and that landlords will not invest if they must deal with tenant unions [S6]. "If we really want to solve the housing crisis, this approach will not get us there," he said [S6].

## Context / Coverage

- **Tenant organizing.** KNKX reported in May 2026 that renters in six Tacoma buildings had voted to form tenant unions since December. Five of those buildings are owned by a California couple, who had not agreed to bargain [S6]. KNKX described the initiative as enshrining the right to form tenant unions, recognizing a union with more than 50% support, and requiring "good faith bargaining" [S6].
- Cascade PBS's Newsfeed summarized the initiative as making "the city responsible for going after noncompliant landlords" and requiring "landlords to meet with tenant unions" [S7].
- **Process.** Under the City Charter, the council may enact or reject a validated initiative but may not change it, and a rejected initiative goes to the next election [S9]. This research did not find a record of the council's action on this initiative.
- This research could not read Pierce County's local voters' pamphlet (piercecountywa.gov returned HTTP 403) or The News Tribune's coverage.

## Axis notes

- `housing`: the measure is a tenant-protection and enforcement package: tenant union recognition and mandatory bargaining, penalties paid to tenants, private enforcement, and rental licensing and inspections [S1][S5]. The opposition argues it will raise rents and make housing "harder to develop," which is the market-supply view [S1][S6].
- `spending`: it requires the city to create a new enforcement program, rental licensing, a complaint process, inspections and a database, funded by fees [S1][S5]. Opponents argue the cost will add to the city's budget shortfall; supporters say fees will cover it [S1][S3].
- `taxes`: no tax is levied; the funding is a per-unit license fee [S1][S5].
- `local-control`: the measure changes city code only and does not shift authority between levels of government [S1].
- `reform`: the statements argue about "dark money" in the 2023 campaign, but the measure does not change election rules [S1].
