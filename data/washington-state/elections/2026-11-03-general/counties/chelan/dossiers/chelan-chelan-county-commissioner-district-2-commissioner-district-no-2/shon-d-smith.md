## Background

Shon D. Smith prefers the Republican Party and lives in Cashmere [S1][S9]. He was elected Chelan County Commissioner for District 2 in 2022 and has chaired the Board of County Commissioners since 2025 [S1]. He won the 2022 general election over Anne Hessburg 16,833 votes (50.41%) to 16,505 (49.43%) [S8], a margin the Wenatchee World described as "a little over 300 votes" [S9]. He is seeking a second four-year term [S9].

He graduated from Cashmere High School in 1989 and from Northwest Nazarene University in 1993 with a business administration degree, worked at Idaho Timber and Dovex Fruit, and founded the Wok-About Grill restaurants in Wenatchee (1997) and Leavenworth (2014) [S1][S4]. He lists 16 boards, including chair of the Chelan-Douglas Health District board, chair pro tem of Link Transit, chair of the Upper Columbia Salmon Recovery Board (2025) and chair of the Chelan County Lodging Tax Advisory Committee [S1].

In the certified August 4, 2026 primary he placed first among District 2 voters with 4,368 votes (46.42%), ahead of Clint Strand (4,248, 45.15%) and Nathanial Helligso (793, 8.43%) [S7].

## Positions

### Experience and representation
- His campaign site argues that "Chelan County Needs an Experienced Commissioner" and that "a new commissioner spends their first few years getting to know the issues" [S3].
- Says rural residents of unincorporated areas need representation and "remain a priority"; he lives in an unincorporated area [S3].

### Taxes and budget
- Lists "Responsible taxation" and "Limited government - No nanny state!" among his priorities [S4].
- Says the county is "always concerned about keeping taxes low while also keeping the roads paved and the bridges in good repair," and that the board is working to bring new industries "to help spread out the tax base" [S5].
- Says the county is "sitting in a very good position": it has "budgeted for deficits" in the past while being "mindful of not abusing our reserve fund," and "at some point soon" will put money back into reserves through economic development [S5].
- Says "the goal is always to spend less than you have" and to keep reserves "healthy enough to sustain us through the unexpected" [S5].

### Housing, land use and growth
- Lists "Reasonable housing for families" and "Personal property rights protection" among his priorities [S4].
- Says land use is "a big topic" in District 2 "as we see orchard land being repurposed" [S5].
- Says balancing tourism growth with residents' needs is "a mounting concern" and that, as Lodging Tax Committee chair, he wants to diversify visitor groups so areas are not "over-loved" [S5].

### Public safety and justice
- Lists support for "the Fire, Sheriff, EMS and First Responders" as a priority [S4].
- Names replacement of the nearly 50-year-old Chelan County Regional Justice Center (jail) as a coming decision and says the county is making "great strides toward building a four-county diversion facility" [S5].

### Forests and natural resources
- Calls for a wood products campus to use material from the forest rather than "let them contribute to more mega fires" [S5].
- Lists "Healthy forests, waters, agriculture and tourism" as a priority [S4].

### Campaign money
- Says "campaign spending is out of control" and that "election donations also give a false representation of who voters support"; says he is not taking campaign donations this year [S5]. His PDC registration uses the mini-reporting option and shows no reports filed as of September 17, 2026 [S6].

### Gender, LGBTQ+ and reproductive policy
- In July 2023, as a health board member, he objected to the phrase "pregnant people" in a Chelan-Douglas Health District wildfire-smoke presentation and asked that it say "pregnant women"; the board chair deflected his request to make it a motion [S18].
- His campaign FAQ says: "I'm not going to let my personal beliefs affect another person's lifestyle ... it's not government's role to tell people how to live or where to live or who to live with" [S5].

### Parents and schools
- No public position found as of 2026-10-08. His campaign site says his family has "been actively involved with the Cashmere schools" [S4].

## Record

- **Leavenworth urban growth area (2023-2024):** In February 2023 the three commissioners unanimously struck Leavenworth's proposed R8 zoning (8,000-square-foot minimum lots) from the city's urban growth area amendments, despite a unanimous "do pass" from the county planning commission [S10]. Smith said: "Increasing the housing in the areas where we should be preserving the ability to have space to roam, at a great expense to us, I think is something that needs to be protected" [S10]. After Leavenworth won an appeal at the Growth Management Hearings Board, the commissioners unanimously adopted the changes in February 2024; Smith called the 1997 county-city memorandum that the board relied on "irrelevant" [S11].
- **Encampment ordinance (2024):** Smith and Commissioner Kevin Overbay voted to adopt an ordinance treating overnight camping on county property without permission as a public nuisance and allowing deputies to trespass campers for five years; Commissioner Tiffany Gering was absent [S12].
- **Budget (2025):** Four motions by Commissioner Brad Hawkins, including one setting "a goal of approving annual balanced budgets for its general fund," died after his fellow commissioners cited confidence in the county's existing process [S13]. In April 2025 the commissioners discussed a deficit-neutral budget after a $4.1 million gap in the 2025 budget [S14].
- **Permit fees (2025):** The commissioners adopted a land-use permit fee schedule phased in over three years, then rising 4% a year from 2029, after the community development department drew $300,000 from the general fund in 2024. Smith said it would help the department become self-sustaining by 2028 and called the phasing "a compromise that works for builders, the public and the county" [S15].
- **Malaga tax increment area (2025):** The commissioners unanimously passed a six-month moratorium on tax increment financing districts in unincorporated areas, citing concerns that the port authority's proposed 3,326-acre Malaga district would affect taxpayers and county services [S16]. The county later offered the port $1.5 million a year for 25 years from existing rural economic development and road revenues in exchange for dropping the plan; the port rejected the offer [S17]. Smith's FAQ calls the Malaga tax increment area "a confusing topic that we worked hard to educate the public about" [S5].
