## Background

Patrick David Murphy lives in Sequim and works in Port Angeles [S1][S6]. He grew up in a Navy family, finished high school in Silverdale, and earned a B.A. in political science from the University of Washington (2011) and a J.D. from Seattle University School of Law (2014). He passed the bar in 2015 [S1][S4]. He practiced privately in Kitsap County for five years and received a Kitsap Law Day award in 2017 for the most pro bono hours with Kitsap Legal Aid [S4]. He joined the Clallam County Prosecuting Attorney's Office criminal division in 2019 [S4][S7]. He reports 11 years as a lawyer in all [S1].

## Record

- Deputy prosecuting attorney for more than seven years, practicing mainly in District Court 1. His caseload has covered domestic violence, crimes against persons, DUI and driving offenses, property crimes, firearms, licensing and drug possession [S1][S2].
- Reports prosecuting more than 3,000 cases and 75-plus jury trials (self-reported) [S2].
- Represents the Salish region on the Trueblood General Advisory Committee, which advises on competency restoration and mental health services. He also teaches sexual-assault investigation protocols [S1][S4].
- Certified tax preparer with the IRS; member of the Washington Association of Prosecuting Attorneys [S1].
- No judicial or pro tem service is reported, so there are no rulings to review.

## Positions

- **New direction.** Says quality of life in Port Angeles and Sequim is at risk from "shuttered businesses, increasing drug use and a rise in quality of life crimes," and that District Court 1 "needs a new, more effective direction" [S1][S3]. At filing he said "there's not enough accountability occurring in District Court" [S6].
- **Release conditions and bail.** Says he does not believe "sufficient release conditions have been set for violent offenses" [S6]. His site names conditions of release, including bail, as "a primary means" of putting firm safeguards in place [S3]. At the Sept. 1 debate he said his opponent does not apply the release factor on the likelihood of violating a no-contact or protection order [S7].
- **Judicial approach.** Describes his philosophy as one that "prioritizes victims and the community while ensuring that the rights of the accused are respected." He says the judge's role is "to be an independent arbiter" and pledges "fair consideration to both sides" [S1][S6][S7].
- **Restorative justice and therapeutic courts.** Calls for "balancing firm accountability with restorative justice practices" and wants to "continue and expand the use of restorative justice and rehabilitation" [S1][S6]. Supports Mental Health Court but says violations should carry consequences. He wants therapeutic courts to follow national and state guidelines more consistently [S3].
- **Public defense.** Says the court has been "laissez faire" in deciding who is indigent. He wants eligibility rules for appointed counsel applied strictly and repayment required when ineligible defendants used a public defender [S7].
- **Homelessness.** His site says he is focused on ways the justice system can help reduce the effects of homelessness [S4].

## Endorsements

Retired Clallam County Sheriff Bill Benedict, retired Undersheriff Ron Cameron, retired Washington State Patrol Sgt. Stephen Robley and Trooper Eric Ellefson, retired Clallam deputies Jim Dixon and Stacy Sampson, and Sarah Barrington, former therapeutic courts manager for District Court 1 [S5]. He says he is not seeking endorsements from practicing Clallam County attorneys or from prosecutors, citing the appearance of fairness [S5].

## Scoring notes

- No judicial or pro tem service. He runs as the change candidate, saying the court needs "a new, more effective direction" [S1][S3].
- His formation is seven-plus years as a District Court prosecutor. His stated priorities lean toward accountability: stricter release conditions for violent offenses, victims first, and strict indigency screening. He pairs them with support for restorative justice and therapeutic courts that sanction violations [S3][S6][S7].
- States no interpretive philosophy in the as-written versus pragmatic sense; his stated philosophy concerns victims, community and defendants' rights [S7].
