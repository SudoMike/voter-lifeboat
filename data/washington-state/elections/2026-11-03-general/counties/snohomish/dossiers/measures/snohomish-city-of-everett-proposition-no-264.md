## What it does

Proposition 264 amends charter Sections 11.1, 11.2, 11.3 and 11.5 on initiatives and referenda. The ballot title: it "would require an initiative or referendum petition be signed by qualified electors in a number equal to ten percent of the total number of votes cast at the last preceding Mayoral election; would allow for 10 days to correct faulty signatures; would streamline the initiative process; and would require the City to prepare a Fiscal Analysis of the measure that would be made available to voters" [S1].

- **Initiative thresholds today.** A petition signed by 15% of votes cast at the last general municipal election obliges the council, within 10 days, to pass the ordinance or send it to a special election within 45 days; a petition with 5% to under 15% goes to the next regular municipal election if the council does not pass it [S2].
- **Initiative threshold proposed.** One threshold: signatures equal to 10% of votes cast at the last Mayoral election. The council then has 30 days after the Clerk's certification to pass the ordinance unaltered or submit it at the next municipal election at least 90 days later (or an earlier special election). The 5% route is deleted [S2].
- **Referendum.** Stays at 10%, but measured against the last Mayoral election rather than the last general municipal election [S2].
- **Process.** Petitions must name a sponsor committee of up to three people; the petition is final on filing; the Clerk has 20 days (now 10) to certify, and the County Auditor may do the count; if short, sponsors get 10 calendar days to re-obtain signatures deemed invalid [S2].
- **Fiscal impact statement.** For an initiative with sufficient signatures the city's Finance Director prepares a fiscal impact statement posted on the city website, and the voters' guide explanatory statement links to it [S2].

## Cost

No tax or rate [S1]. Adds a Finance Director fiscal impact statement for qualifying initiatives [S2].

## Fiscal mechanics / What it replaces

Replaces the two-tier (15% / 5%) initiative thresholds and the general-municipal-election base with a single 10% Mayoral-election base [S2].

## Arguments for

From the statement written by Mason Rutledge and Brent Terry [S1]:

- "Proposition 264 establishes a single standard for petitions and clarifies the procedures for submitting, reviewing, and processing petitions." (The statement describes the base as "the most recent general election"; the ballot title and charter text say the last Mayoral election [S1][S2].)
- "The proposition would require a fiscal impact statement for measures that qualify for the ballot."
- "It means clearer rules, better accountability, transparency, and a healthier civic culture."

## Arguments against

No statement against was filed. The pamphlet says no person in the jurisdiction contacted the Auditor's office to provide a statement [S1].

## Context / Coverage

- **How it got here.** Everett's charter (adopted 1968) is reviewed every 10 years by a Charter Review Committee; the City Council adopted the committee's recommendations, with its own changes, in Ordinance No. 4189-26, passed July 15, 2026, sending Propositions 261-265 to the ballot [S1][S2]. The Herald reports the council forwarded most of the 15-member committee's recommendations, rejected a proposal to review the charter every five years instead of ten, and voted 5-2 against adding term limits, which the committee had not advanced [S3].
- **Passage.** Simple majority of Everett voters; ratified amendments take effect on the date of the county's certification of the election [S2].
- **Coverage.** The Herald says the change raises the signatures needed for initiatives to about 2,200 (10% of the last mayoral election), "up from the previous requirement of 5% of the total votes cast in the last general election," and lowers the referendum threshold from 15% to 10% [S3]. The charter text shows the referendum threshold is already 10% today (Section 11.3) and 15% is the initiative tier that forces council action [S2]; this dossier follows the charter text.
