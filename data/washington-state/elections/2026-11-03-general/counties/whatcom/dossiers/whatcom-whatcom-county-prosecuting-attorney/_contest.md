## What the office does
The county prosecuting attorney decides whether people arrested by police are charged with a felony or misdemeanor, decides who is eligible for diversion programs, makes sentencing recommendations and leads plea negotiations [S2]. The prosecutor is also the lead lawyer when Whatcom County is sued and advises the County Council and county departments [S2][S3]. Candidates must live in the county and be admitted to the state bar; the term is four years [S1][S2]. The office has about 70 staff [S4].

## Race dynamics
The seat is open: Prosecutor Eric Richey, who succeeded his mentor David McEachran's 40-year tenure, leaves at the end of his second term [S2][S4]. Four candidates ran in the August primary, the first time since the 1930s, and the two who advanced are Ryan Swinburnson, a defense attorney who directs the county's conflict defense office and states no party preference, and Jesse Corkern, a Democrat and senior civil deputy prosecutor in the office [S3][S7]. Swinburnson led the primary with 38.4% as of the Thursday count; the three Democrats from inside the office were within 3 points of one another [S5].

The campaign centers on the office itself. CDN counted 49 staff departures from 2019 through 2025, including 22 attorneys, and reports cases two to four years old and a new Superior Court calendar for aging cases; Richey says the office is now fully staffed [S4]. A CDN report in August described HR complaints involving the two in-office candidates who were eliminated in the primary, and Richey's decision against an outside investigation [S6]. Both finalists propose fixes: Corkern would add attorney specialization, consistent charging standards and merit-based leadership; Swinburnson would add mentorship, supervisors in court and early plea offers (jesse-corkern.md S1, S5; ryan-swinburnson.md S4, S6). On crime, Swinburnson leads with accountability for violent and repeat offenders and drug dealers and adds therapeutic courts including a Veterans Court; Corkern pairs zero-tolerance policies for gun violence with a Community Court for non-violent offenders (ryan-swinburnson.md S1, S4; jesse-corkern.md S1, S3). Corkern has Democratic Party, labor and Democratic legislators' endorsements; Swinburnson lists Rep. Alicia Rule, local mayors and council members, school superintendents and current and retired police leaders (jesse-corkern.md S4; ryan-swinburnson.md S5). Corkern has raised about $78,000 to Swinburnson's $42,000 (jesse-corkern.md S9; ryan-swinburnson.md S11). Neither has a stated position on the 2026 statewide initiatives.

## Differentiating issue axes
1. **Safety:** both pair enforcement with treatment; Swinburnson's emphasis is accountability for violent and repeat offenders and drug dealers, Corkern's is zero tolerance for gun violence plus a Community Court for non-violent offenders.
2. **Experience:** an outside defense attorney who holds elected school-board office and stresses "experienced leadership," against a first-time candidate from inside the office running on "A New Kind of Leadership."
3. **Office management (no rubric axis):** turnover, training, charging standards and case delays are the main campaign subject for both.
4. **Party label:** a no-party-preference candidate against a Democrat with party and labor endorsements; this does not map cleanly to the reform axis and is not scored.
