## What it does

Proposition 2026-05 is a multi-year levy lid lift for the City of Lynden's regular property tax [S1]. It would [S1]:
- set the city's levy for collection in 2027 at a maximum of $1.54304 per $1,000 of assessed value, which the city says is $0.50 more than the 2025 levy rate;
- allow the levy to grow 3% a year (a limit factor of 100% plus 3%) for 2027 through 2035;
- make the 2035 levy amount the base for later limits.

**Stated purposes** [S1]: (1) police and fire; (2) street and park maintenance; (3) the Lynden Community/Senior Center; (4) restoring staff positions cut in 2025 and 2026; (5) reopening City Hall to the public on Fridays.

The City Council voted 5-1 on July 6, 2026 to place it on the ballot. Nick Laninga voted no and Lee Beld was absent [S3][S5]. The council adopted Resolution No. 26-1148 that day and amended the ballot language with Resolution No. 26-1152 on August 3 to meet the word limit [S2]. Passage needs a simple majority [S2][S5].

## Cost

- The city's current rate is about $1.04 per $1,000 [S2]. The measure raises it to $1.54304 per $1,000 in 2027 [S1], an increase of about 48% in the city's rate (computed for this dossier).
- The city's figure: about $25 a month, or $300 a year, more on a $600,000 home [S2][S3].
- The city says the increase would raise about $2 million a year on 2026 assessed value of about $4.0 billion, on top of the roughly $4.1 million the current levy raises [S2][S3].
- After 2027 the levy amount may grow 3% a year through 2035 [S1].
- The city's share is about 13.6% of the total property tax on a typical $600,000 Lynden home ($623.41 of $4,588.31), per the city [S2].

## Fiscal mechanics / What it replaces

This is a new increase, not a renewal of an expiring levy [S1][S2]. Without voter approval, the city's regular levy can grow only 1% a year [S1][S2]. The city says its rate fell from $2.14 per $1,000 in 2014 to $1.04 in 2026 as values rose: home values rose about 2.6 times while the tax collected on a house rose about 1.3 times [S2]. Mayor Scott Korthuis said "we are collecting only one-third of the property tax today compared to what we collected in 2002" [S5].

**What led here, per the city and news coverage:**
- The city cut 10 positions, about 10% of its workforce, in the 2025 and 2026 budgets and closed City Hall to the public on Fridays [S2][S3][S4].
- Last year the council passed a new 0.1% public safety sales tax [S3].
- Sales tax revenue has been flat for two years; real estate excise tax fell from more than $1 million in 2022 to $600,000 last year; building fees are down by about half [S2][S5].
- The city's general fund has had less than 30 days of operating reserves, below the state's 60-day recommendation [S2].
- The city cites unfunded state and county mandates, including indigent defense standards, Phase II stormwater rules, and county-assigned basic life support ambulance service that grew into a full-time fire department costing over $4 million a year [S2]. Lynden's share of WhatComm 911 costs is expected to grow from $168,000 to $351,000, and its county emergency management share from $42,000 to $112,000 [S5].
- Whatcom County has discussed cutting funding for senior centers; Lynden's center received $174,000 over the last two years [S3].

**The city's spending plan** [S2]:
- $300,000 a year to reserves.
- Police: replace seven patrol cars (about $80,000 each), add two officers, and restore a records clerk plus a half-time position for body-camera records requests.
- Fire: four full-time firefighters to reduce overtime, then more staff toward a third ambulance; the city says matching call demand would take 14 more fire staff over 10 years. The fire department ran more than 2,700 calls last year, about 85% for ambulance service.
- Restore cut positions in finance, public works, parks, community development, IT and the clerk's office.

The city says it considered joining a fire district but that this would cost residents more than the $0.50 levy; nearby small cities in fire districts pay $0.61 to $1.90 per $1,000 for fire service [S2]. Korthuis said he first considered asking for $1.00 and cutting the ambulance utility fee, but the council settled on $0.50 [S5].

**If it fails:** the city says it would cut staff and services in parks, police and fire [S2]. Council member Gary Vis said the options are "fewer police officers, fewer firemen or fewer parks" [S3]. Korthuis said the 2027 budget would be written assuming no new money and that some cuts are likely regardless [S5].

## Arguments for

The statement for (Committee For Lynden Proposition #1: Kevin Pawlowski, Jonathan Abramson and Robert Libolt) says [S1]:
- "Belt-tightening measures have been implemented throughout all Lynden City Departments," but the city cannot raise what it needs to keep services.
- Population growth requires more police officers, and the fire department handles over 2,700 calls a year.
- "For only a small additional amount of 50 cents per $1,000 of assessed value," the city can keep police, fire, streets, parks and the community center.

Council member Brent Lenssen said that when property tax can grow only 1% and costs rise 4%, "do the math" [S3]. Council member Gary Bode, who said he was "hesitant" given state and federal tax increases, voted to place it on the ballot: "Out of all the forced taxes we get, this is one where we get to choose" [S3][S5].

## Arguments against

No statement against was filed: "No person in the jurisdiction contacted the Auditor's Office to provide a statement" [S1]. No committee against was formed [S1], and the PDC query found no committee for or against this measure [S6].
- Council member Nick Laninga voted against placing it on the ballot; no explanation was reported [S3][S5].
- CDN notes Lynden voters have recently been reluctant to approve tax increases, rejecting several Lynden School District requests in the last two years [S3].
- Bode noted the burden of rising state and federal taxes on Lynden residents [S3][S5].
