## What it does

Initiative 26-01 is a citizen initiative that adds a new chapter to the Bellingham Municipal Code banning algorithmic price-fixing in the rental housing market [S1]. It would make it unlawful [S1][S2]:
- for two or more landlords to agree, in writing, verbally or by conduct, to set rental prices;
- for a landlord to pay for a "coordinating service";
- for a provider to sell coordinating services to two or more landlords.

A "coordinating service" collects rent, occupancy or lease data from two or more landlords or from databases and uses an algorithm to recommend rents, lease renewal terms or occupancy levels to more than one landlord [S2]. Publishing rent estimates based only on public data that anyone can get free of contract is excluded [S2].

**Still allowed:** recordkeeping software; aggregated market reports that do not recommend future rents or terms; and data used for project financing or appraisals [S1][S2]. Hotels, motels and short-term rentals are not covered [S1][S2].

**Enforcement** [S1][S2]:
- A person injured by a violation may sue for damages of up to $5,000 per violation, three times actual damages, and attorney fees. Each affected unit and each year can count as a separate violation.
- The City Attorney may investigate and act on complaints.
- A first offense is a civil infraction with a fine of up to $5,000 per violation. (The initiative text applies this subsection to "any employer or their agent violating the requirement under this chapter" [S2].)
- A second or willful violation may be a misdemeanor, with up to $5,000 and 90 days in jail.
- Tenants and employees who report violations get anti-retaliation and whistleblower protections; an employee shown to have faced retaliation is entitled to $7,500 in damages plus fees [S2].
- The text says state and federal law apply if they conflict with it, and it has a severability clause [S2].

**How it reached the ballot.** Community First Whatcom began gathering signatures in April 2026 [S3]. On June 26 organizers delivered almost 6,000 signatures to City Hall; 3,500 valid signatures from city voters were required [S4]. The initiative is modeled on Seattle's and Portland's laws [S4]. The initiative's findings say it is "substantially similar to protections adopted in Seattle and King County" [S2].

## Cost

- No tax, levy or fee [S1]. The voters' guide has no fiscal statement [S1].
- Enforcement is through private lawsuits and the City Attorney; the initiative creates no new city office or funding source [S1][S2]. No city estimate of enforcement cost was found.

## Fiscal mechanics / What it replaces

No Bellingham law on rental pricing software exists today; the initiative adds a new chapter [S1].

**Context reported by CDN** [S3][S4]:
- In 2025 the Washington Attorney General sued RealPage, a pricing software provider, and several King County landlords for antitrust violations.
- A 2026 state Senate bill to ban algorithmic price-fixing, sponsored by Sens. Liz Lovelett and Sharon Shewmake, did not make it out of committee.
- A 2024 White House Council of Economic Advisers report estimated algorithmic price-fixing cost U.S. renters about $3.8 billion in 2023; the report has since been removed from the White House website.

**Is it happening in Bellingham?** The initiative's own findings say algorithmic price-fixing "is currently not known to be a common practice in Bellingham, meaning most local providers will not need to expend resources to be in compliance" [S2]. Organizer Selena Knoblauch told CDN it is hard to know whether it is happening in a given market and called the measure an "affirmative defense" [S4]. The campaign FAQ says landlords do not have to report using such software, and the whistleblower provisions are meant to help detect it [S2].

## Arguments for

The statement for (Community First Whatcom: Seth Mangold, Eukia Lemaster and Finn Kurtz) says [S1]:
- Pricing tools pool data from subscribing landlords and "have the ability to increase the cost of living by suggesting that clients raise their prices collectively. When done by people, this is illegal price-fixing. Automation should not be a loophole to federal law."
- Algorithmically priced units are on average "$70/month more expensive," citing nationwide reporting.
- The model has been used in Seattle, Portland, Philadelphia, Minneapolis and Washington, D.C.

The campaign FAQ adds that the initiative "does not set any new limits to the prices landlords are allowed to set" [S2]. Chair Cleveland Harris II told CDN that once one property manager uses the software it can give other landlords cover to raise rents [S4].

## Arguments against

No statement against was filed: "No person in the jurisdiction contacted the Auditor's Office to provide a statement" [S1]. No committee against was formed [S1], and the PDC query found no committee opposing it [S6].

A July 2026 CDN guest column by Kevin Donnelly, executive director of the Real Estate Technology and Transformation Center in Washington, D.C., argued against the ban [S5]:
- Rents are high because "demand is high and supply is relatively low," and a ban does not add housing.
- Pricing software "often" recommends rent decreases in response to market conditions.
- He contrasted San Francisco, which banned the software in October 2024 and where he says rents rose, with Austin, where it is legal and rents fell, and credited Austin's zoning and building-code changes.
- He called the measure "well-intentioned" but "a solution that doesn't solve," and urged Bellingham to loosen height, floor-area, lot-size and inclusionary-zoning rules instead.
