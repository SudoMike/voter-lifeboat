## What it does

Proposition 2026-06 is a proposed amendment to the Bellingham city charter [S1]. It deletes the charter's requirement that "the Mayor's salary should at no time be less than that of the highest paid City official or employee" and gives the city's independent salary commission the job of setting the mayor's salary [S1].

- The salary commission was created by ordinance to periodically review and fix City Council salaries [S1]. The council created it in 2021, and it reviews council pay every eight years [S2].
- The charter was drafted by freeholders in 1972 [S2]. The amendment came from the mayor's office, and the City Council voted unanimously on July 13, 2026 to send it to voters [S2].
- The municipal code currently says the mayor must earn 5% more than the highest-paid department head; that code would also need to be revised [S2].
- The explanatory statement notes that Whatcom County and the State of Washington both use citizen salary commissions for elected officials [S1].

## Cost

- The measure sets no tax and no salary figure [S1]. The voters' guide has no fiscal statement [S1].
- Mayor Kim Lund makes just under $237,000 a year plus $49,128 in benefits, which CDN reports is more than the Seattle mayor's roughly $230,000 [S2]. Lund declined the 3% cost-of-living increase for 2026 [S2].
- Bellingham council members make about $73,000 a year in 2026 [S2].
- Whether the mayor's pay would go up or down under a commission is not stated in any source found; the commission would decide [S1].

## Fiscal mechanics / What it replaces

Today the mayor's pay is tied by formula to the highest-paid city employee [S1]. The explanatory statement says the city sets employee and department-head salaries "based on market factors related to recruitment and retention," and those increases "automatically increase the Mayor's salary" [S1]. CDN describes "cascading salary adjustments": labor contracts raise supervisors' and managers' pay along with their staff, and the mayor's pay follows [S2].

Council member Lisa Anderson said the link makes it "increasingly very difficult to hire really high caliber people" without further raising the mayor's pay [S2]. A March 2026 guest column said the charter section "has perversely hampered a nationwide search for a new city attorney" [S3].

**The salary commission debate.** Two March 2026 guest columns in CDN discussed local salary commissions before this measure was proposed:
- Andrew Reding, a 2025 Whatcom County Charter Review Commission member, wrote that commission decisions are final and unelected, that they had pushed local salaries up, and that Bellingham should repeal the charter's mayor-pay section and add "guardrails" on the city salary commission [S3].
- RB Tewksbury, a member of the 2022 Bellingham Salary Commission, replied that the city commission "has absolutely no authority over the mayor's salary," which is set by the charter formula, and that it meets only once every eight years to set council pay [S4].

## Arguments for

The statement for (committee: Kim Lund and Edwin "Skip" Williams) says [S1]:
- The current formula drives the mayor's pay through "salary compression" as wages rise through the city workforce.
- A salary commission is "the same transparent process" used for the City Council and for elected officials across Whatcom County.
- No similarly sized or larger Washington city it reviewed sets the mayor's salary in relation to staff salaries; "Bellingham's current method is the lone outlier."
- A commission "allows the public direct conversation about compensation for elected officials, rather than leaving it to an indirect formula."

Council member Michael Lilliquist said "it took a mayor to bring it forward" [S2].

## Arguments against

No statement against was filed: "No person in the jurisdiction contacted the Auditor's Office to provide a statement" [S1]. No committee against was formed [S1].

A related critique, not directed at this measure: Reding wrote that salary commissions' decisions are final, that "no one argues the other side," and that there is "no real public accountability" [S3]. He proposed tying elected officials' pay to a multiple of the state minimum wage instead [S3].
