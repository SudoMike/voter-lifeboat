## What it does

Proposition No. 1 is a levy lid lift. It would raise the City of Langley's regular property tax rate by $0.648544721 to a total of $1.66 per $1,000 of assessed value, for collection in 2027 "and annually thereafter" [S1]. The council adopted Resolution 877 to place it on the ballot [S1].

- **Permanent.** The ballot title says it would "permanently increase" the rate [S1]. State law lets a lid lift's higher levy become the base for later limits if the ballot says so [S14]. After 2027 the levy would again grow by the normal 1% a year plus new construction [S1][S3].
- **Purpose.** The ballot title says it is to "provide for continual governmental services and update the City's operating and electronic technology." The money "will be used to fund general City services" [S1].
- **Senior and disability exemption.** The measure provides for the exemption under RCW 84.36.381 [S1].
- **Room under the cap.** The city says its maximum rate is about $2.08 after the fire and library district rates are subtracted, so about $1.07 is available. This measure uses about $0.65 of it [S2]. DOR lists Langley's 2025 maximum rate as $2.08417 [S8].
- The council first set a preliminary rate of about $1.74 on July 6, then adopted $1.66 on July 20, 2026 [S5][S6]. In June it had considered increases of $0.38, $0.42 or $0.84 [S4].

## Cost

- **Current rate:** $1.0106789 per $1,000 [S1].
- **New rate:** $1.66 per $1,000, an increase of $0.648544721 [S1]. That is about a 64% increase in the city's rate (computed for this dossier from the ballot figures).
- **Average home:** the explanatory statement says about $42 a month in 2027 for the average residence, valued in 2026 at $770,000 [S1]. The city's FAQ gives the same example as $500.50 a year [S2]. Computed for this dossier from the exact rate: $770,000 × $0.648544721 / 1,000 = about $499 a year more. The city portion of the bill would go from about $778 to about $1,278.
- **$500,000 home:** about $324 a year more (computed for this dossier).
- **Revenue:** the Record reported the new rate would raise the city's annual property tax collections from about $492,000 to about $808,000 [S6]. At the 2025 assessed value of $481,334,830, the added rate would raise about $312,000 (computed for this dossier from DOR data [S8]; 2027 values will differ).
- **Total bills, as the two sides describe them.** The statement against says the total property tax on a $770,000 median home would go from $6,300 to $6,800 [S1]. The Record, citing city estimates, put the average homeowner's current total bill at about $4,505 [S5]. This research did not reconcile the two figures.

## Fiscal mechanics / What it replaces

**Levy history.** Department of Revenue levy detail shows a large jump in Langley's rate in 2006, then a long fall as assessed values rose faster than the 1% limit [S8][S9][S10][S11][S12][S13]:

| Taxes due | Assessed value | Rate per $1,000 | City regular levy |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2005 | $174,030,340 | $1.04510 | $181,879 [S13] |
| 2006 | $178,285,711 | $1.83000 | $326,263 [S12] |
| 2015 | $235,487,493 | $1.69751 | $399,742 [S11] |
| 2020 | $313,358,365 | $1.40584 | $440,531 [S10] |
| 2024 | $475,484,646 | $1.00034 | $475,646 [S9] |
| 2025 | $481,334,830 | $1.00935 | $485,836 [S8] |

The 2005-to-2006 jump fits a voter-approved lid lift, and the city says it has not asked voters for one "for more than 20 years" [S1]. This research did not find the earlier ballot record. The years in the table are samples.

**The budget gap, as the city describes it.**

- The 2026 budget projects general fund spending of $2,538,382 against revenue of $2,438,278. The $100,104 gap is covered from fund balance [S3].
- The projected 2026 ending general fund balance is $235,772. The city's own minimum is $632,166 [S3]. In June 2026 the Record reported the city expected to be about $200,000 short of its minimum by year end [S4].
- The budget memo says that since 2001 "inflation has grown 90%" while the city's "buying power has only grown 25%" [S3]. The 1% limit let the city add $4,835.82 in property tax for 2026 [S3].
- General fund spending exceeded revenue from 2021 to 2023, and pandemic money covered the gap [S4].
- Finance Director Kelly Beech said that without the lift the city would use up all general fund reserves by 2028 [S6].

**Cuts and other revenue already used.** The city cut passport services, closed City Hall to walk-ins on Thursdays and Fridays, delayed a software upgrade, and left the city administrator job unfilled [S2]. It left a fourth police officer position unfilled [S6]. It adopted a public safety sales tax (budgeted at $59,143 in 2026) and a transportation benefit district sales tax ($71,118) [S2].

**What the money would pay for.** The city lists as underfunded: administrative staff, payroll liability for departing employees, emergency preparedness, litigation, technology, vehicle and equipment replacement, and facility maintenance [S2]. Council members focused on hiring a full-time city administrator and replacing municipal software [S4][S5]. The mayor has been doing much of the administrator's work [S4][S5].

**Public records lawsuits.** The city says $207,000 of its 2026 legal costs relate to Public Records Act lawsuits filed in 2023 and 2025 [S2]. In August 2026 the council amended the 2026 budget, adding more than $100,000 to the legal budget [S7]. The mayor said suits by one resident had cost over $119,000 since November 2025 [S7].

**More lifts later.** Mayor Kennedy Horstman and Beech said this is likely not the last lid lift and that new lifts will need to be considered every year. "Lifts aren't a sign of failure," Horstman said [S6].

**Disincorporation claim (verified with caveats).** The statement for says that without the measure the general fund is "projected to run a deficit by 2028, forcing the city to consider unincorporating" [S1]. The record:

- July 2026: Horstman "warned Langley could eventually be forced to disincorporate" if the measure fails and finances keep deteriorating [S6].
- June 2026: the Record reported that Horstman "stressed that would not be the outcome of the levy lid lift's potential failure" and noted that it is unusual for cities to unincorporate [S4].
- Under state law, disincorporation needs a petition signed by a majority of the city's registered voters, then an election. It passes only if a majority of the registered voters voting at that election vote for dissolution [S15]. Failure of this levy would not disincorporate the city.

## Arguments for

From the statement for, credited in the voters' guide to the City of Langley [S1]:

- The city has not asked for a lid lift in more than 20 years. Inflation and unfunded state mandates "can no longer be avoided with band-aids" [S1].
- "This is not a budget expansion; it is a necessary step to fund ongoing essential services" [S1].
- Without it, the general fund runs a deficit by 2028, "forcing the city to consider unincorporating" [S1].
- City officials "are our neighbors, accountable to us and reachable with a walk to City Hall, not a distant county office. Let's not hand local decisions to Island County" [S1].

The city's FAQ adds that contracting police to the sheriff would cost about the same or more, and that state law bars using lodging tax for general services [S2]. Councilmember Chris Carlson said "South Whidbey needs us to be a city" for housing and services [S4]. He also said the city should be "conservative in our ask" because of the cost to residents on fixed incomes [S5].

## Arguments against

From the statement against, filed by the Committee for Fair Tax Increase in Langley [S1]:

- It is "a 65% city property tax increase" and "just too large" [S1].
- "We are paying more to duplicate services that could be provided by Island County at lower cost" [S1].
- The city spends about $230,000 on tourism promotion. "Over 2/3 of the levy increase could be eliminated by shifting money from tourism to our general fund" [S1].
- "We are ALL FOR a tax increase, but not this large." The authors ask the council to return with a smaller request [S1].

Context on these claims:

- The 2026 budget shows the Tourism Fund at $207,542 in projected spending, paid from lodging tax revenue [S3]. The city says state law limits lodging tax to tourism promotion and tourism facilities [S2]. The budget memo says the city is exploring using some lodging tax for affordable housing [S3].
- The city says contracting with the county for permitting is not possible and that a sheriff's contract would not save money [S2].

## Endorsements and campaign

- **For:** the PDC lists "Langley Citizens for the Levy," registered September 17, 2026, treasurer Christopher Dean Carlson. It uses mini reporting and had no reports on file [S16]. Langley has a councilmember named Chris Carlson [S4][S5]. This research did not confirm they are the same person.
- **Against:** no PDC committee named "Committee for Fair Tax Increase in Langley" was found [S16]. The PDC lists the City of Langley as a jurisdiction where "no reporting required" applies to candidates, given its roughly 1,071 registered voters [S16].
- No endorsements from outside groups were found.

## Lean notes

- `taxes`: a yes vote raises the city levy rate about 64%, from $1.01 to $1.66 per $1,000, and keeps the higher base permanently [S1][S14]. A levy lid lift is the cap-lift example at the rubric's pole_b. The "no" side says it supports a smaller increase [S1], so a no vote is not a stance against any increase.
- `spending`: mixed, no mapping. The city frames the measure as restoring core services and filling an unfunded administrator job, "not a budget expansion" [S1][S2]. Opponents argue for moving money from tourism instead [S1], which the city says state law limits [S2].
- `local-control`: some evidence. The statement for frames a yes vote as keeping decisions in a small city rather than handing them "to Island County" [S1]. The statement against says the county could provide the same services more cheaply [S1]. That is the rubric's local-unit versus county-level tension (yes leans pole_a, local control). But the measure does not shift authority itself. Disincorporation would need a separate petition and vote [S15], and the mayor has said failure would not lead to it [S4]. Any mapping here is weak and rests on the campaign arguments, not on what the measure does.
- `social` / `parental-rights`: not addressed.
