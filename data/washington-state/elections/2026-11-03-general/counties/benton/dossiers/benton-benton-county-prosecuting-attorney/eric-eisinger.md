## Background

Eric Eisinger has been Benton County Prosecuting Attorney since 2023 (S1). He won the office in 2022 over Ryan Lukson, also a Republican, 51% to 48% (S7). Before that he was a senior partner at the Richland firm Walker Heye (2010-2022) and a City of Kennewick prosecutor (2006-2010), and he served as a designated drug task force prosecutor in 2005-2006 (S1, S2). He reports more than 22 years of litigation experience, was president of the Benton Franklin Counties Bar Association (2016-2017) and sits on the board of the Washington Association of Prosecuting Attorneys (S1). He holds a J.D. from the University of Idaho (2003) and a B.A. from Washington State University (1999) (S1). He prefers the Republican Party (S1).

In the August 4 primary he received 22,046 votes (54.1%) to Julie Long's 18,218 (44.7%) (S8).

## Positions

### Public safety and charging
- His statement says "Olympia continues to push policies that put criminals ahead of victims," and that he will not let Benton County "become the next failed experiment in soft-on-crime policies." He pledges "appropriate bail and punishment" (S1).
- Says he has put policies in place to "charge the highest provable offense justified by the facts, including firearm and deadly weapon enhancements" (S2).
- Says he has "aggressively moved juvenile cases to adult court" for serious offenses, including juvenile homicide cases (S2).
- Appointed a designated major property crime prosecutor (S2, S4).
- Says he worked with the Sheriff's Office to remove a trailer and tent encampment near Dallas Road (S2).
- Supports Veterans Court, Drug Court, Recovery Court, Mental Health Court and the Columbia Valley Recovery Center, saying he believes "in second chances where it's appropriate if an offender is willing to put in the work" (S2).
- In a July interview he said "the most important person in a criminal case is the victim" and that the state majority party "is pushing defendants rights way too far" (S3).

### Office budget and state funding
- Said lower public defender caseload limits shifted costs to the county while the state cut crime victim funding, so "the county is now having to look at additional funding for crime victim services" (S3).
- Said the office needs roughly twice its current number of criminal prosecutors (about 30) to handle cases at an ideal level, and that "the state is failing to fund the local criminal justice system" (S4).

### Firearms and the sheriff's office
- "I will not prosecute any case that infringes upon your right to keep and bear arms" (S2).
- Says he joined a lawsuit against the State of Washington "to defend your constitutional right to independently elect your County Sheriff" (S2).

## Record

- When he took office in January 2023 the office had five deputy prosecutor vacancies; by September 2026 it had one, which he attributes to summer internships and lateral-hire recruiting (S4).
- Says the office inherited about 360 backlogged adult felony cases in 2023 and has reduced the number, though some cases still await screening. He says juvenile and district court cases are current (S4).
- Has sped up case handling by charging enhancements up front and assigning specialized prosecutors to some crime categories (S4).
- Secured the maximum sentence in State v. Mason Ashby, the Kamiakin shooting-threat case (S2).
- Announced his re-election bid on February 6, 2026, citing leadership-building in the office, stronger ties with law enforcement and a focus on juvenile violent crime (S5).
